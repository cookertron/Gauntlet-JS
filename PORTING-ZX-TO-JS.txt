==============================================================================
             FROM TAPE TO BROWSER: A FIELD MANUAL FOR FAITHFULLY
              REPRODUCING A ZX SPECTRUM GAME FROM ITS .TZX IMAGE
==============================================================================

                 Case study: Chuckie Egg (A&F Software, 1984)
                    Written for an AI model doing this work.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
0. WHAT THIS IS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

0.1 THE JOB

You have been handed a .tzx or .tap file and told to produce a faithful
browser port of the game on it: a single self-contained HTML/JavaScript
file that behaves like the original. This document is your operating
procedure. It tells you what to do, in what sequence, what evidence to
demand of yourself at each step, and -- at length -- what you will get
wrong if you are careless.

Everything here was learned by doing it once, all the way through, to a
1984 single-screen platform game. That project is the case study. Real
addresses and real measured numbers from it appear throughout, because a
method described only in the abstract is unusable. When you see "$B34C" or
"406 T-states" or "12 eggs per level", read it as "in the case study" --
illustrations of a shape, never constants you should expect to find in
your game. Section 8 is devoted entirely to detecting the places where
your game will not be like the case study, and what to do instead. The
case study was an unusually easy specimen in almost every dimension:
unprotected tape, one load, no compression, no paging, no scrolling, no
self-modifying code, a hand-written 48K binary with a third-party
disassembly already available. Assume none of that.

0.2 WHAT YOU ARE PRODUCING

  - A reimplementation, not a transliteration and not an emulator. You are
    writing new code in a new language that produces the same behaviour.
  - Extracted data assets (level maps, sprite bitmaps, spawn tables, sound
    parameters) recovered from the binary, not redrawn by eye.
  - A verification record: which original routine each rule came from, how
    it was checked, what is still unknown.

0.3 WHAT THIS EXCLUDES

This document is about FAITHFUL REPRODUCTION ONLY. It says nothing about
what you might add on top -- decorative scenery, rewind, replay ghosts,
attract modes, particle effects, provenance viewers, difficulty options.
Those are a separate layer and a separate discipline. Two rules about them
belong here anyway:

  - Build the faithful engine first, complete and verified, before adding
    anything.
  - If you do add a layer, make the boundary an ASSERTION and not an
    intention. In the case study a test ran 400 frames with the enhancement
    layer on and off and required the resulting simulation state to be
    BYTE-IDENTICAL. Intent is not a boundary. A test is.

0.4 DECIDE THE SCOPE OF "FAITHFUL" BEFORE YOU START

"The game" is not just the play loop. Decide, in writing, and put the
answer in the README, whether the port reproduces:

    loading screen and border effects during load
    title / attract screen and its tune
    the instructions screen
    key redefinition and joystick options
    high-score table and name entry
    2-player alternation
    the level rotation and the difficulty passes after it

These are cheap to skip and expensive to bolt on later, because several of
them change the engine's shape. In the case study the name-entry screen
switches to IM 1 at $A8A0 and then HALTs at $A8B0 waiting on the ROM's own
KSTATE/LAST_K handling ($5C04, $5C08) -- a completely different input and
timing model from the free-running gameplay loop, inside the same binary.
And the difficulty passes are real rules in code, not folklore: the
ostrich divider at $736B reloads with 2 instead of 3 once cleared_levels
reaches $20, and for 8 <= cleared_levels < 16 the spawn load skips
ostriches entirely. If you scope them out, say so; if you scope them in,
they are recovered the same way as everything else.

0.5 LEGAL AND ETHICAL POSITION

Be straight about this before you start, because it changes what you ship.

  - The game code and data are copyrighted works. Reverse-engineering for
    study and interoperability is broadly defensible; redistributing the
    original binary, or your extracted copies of its levels, sprites and
    tunes, is redistribution of the work.
  - The Spectrum ROM is a separate copyrighted work (Amstrad's, distributed
    freely for emulation under a permission grant, but still not yours to
    relicense). USING A REAL ROM IMAGE LOCALLY IN YOUR HARNESS IS A
    DIFFERENT ACT FROM SHIPPING IT IN THE ARTIFACT. If the game calls ROM
    routines at runtime, use the real ROM locally so your measurements are
    real, and ship neither the ROM nor a transcription of its behaviour
    (see 3.7 and 6.2).
  - Therefore: the DEFAULT is that your extracted assets and your built
    artifact are for the person who owns the tape and asked you to do this.
    Do not publish the built file, do not push it to a public host, and do
    not commit extracted asset dumps to a public repository, unless the
    person directing you has the rights and tells you to. State this
    position once, early, in the project's own README, so that nobody has
    to guess later.
  - Your METHOD, your notes, your tooling and your engine source minus the
    embedded assets are yours and are publishable.
  - Credit the original authors by name in the artifact. If you used a
    third-party disassembly, credit it and send your corrections back.

0.6 PREREQUISITES

Install these before line one. The case study used, and this manual
assumes:

    Python 3
    skoolkit          the Z80 simulator library (SkoolKit's Simulator;
                      it also ships a contended CMIOSimulator, and
                      tap2sna.py for the snapshot route)
    z80dis            the instruction decoder your disassembler wraps
    Pillow, numpy     contact sheets, pixel diffs, ink censuses
    Node              the engine's own language, headless tests,
                      rendered-audio and filmstrip tools

Also get, locally:

    The TZX format specification (the block-by-block field layouts). The
    case study kept TZX_Format.html in the repo. Without it, "write your
    own parser, it is 200 lines" is false -- it is a research task.
    A full emulator with a debugger (Fuse or ZEsarUX) for the protected /
    compressed route, for snapshot export, and as a second opinion on
    frames.
    A real 48K ROM image, LOCAL ONLY, for the same reasons.

0.7 HOW TO READ THIS, AND WHAT TO DO IF YOU HAVE TWO DAYS

Sections 1, 2 and 4 are load-bearing. Section 4, the failure catalogue, is
the longest deliberately: this work fails by producing something
plausible, not by producing something broken, and the catalogue is a list
of the specific ways plausibility lies to you. Read it before phase 5, and
reread it whenever a result surprises you.

Do not execute this document in linear order until you have run the
battery in section 8. Sixteen phases in order, with no diagnostics, is how
you spend a week on format archaeology for a game whose levels are
compressed in RAM.

IF YOU ONLY DO SIX THINGS, do these, in this order. Everything else in the
manual is amplification.

    1. Parse the tape and build the post-loader 64K image (phases 1-2).
    2. Build the simulator harness and boot to a live level (phase 7-8).
       This is the pivot. Nothing before it is verifiable and nothing
       after it is guesswork.
    3. Run the pre-harness and post-harness batteries (section 8) and fill
       in the decision checklist at 8.20.
    4. Measure the clock (phase 9). Get this wrong and every other number
       is wrong by the same factor.
    5. Read the main loop, the divider chain and the movement routines,
       and write the spec with addresses (phase 10).
    6. Write the engine mirroring the dispatch structure, with one
       differential test at the busiest call boundary (phases 12-13).

Static asset extraction (phase 5) can always be replaced by reading the
data out of live RAM in the harness. The harness cannot be replaced by
anything.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE PRIME DIRECTIVE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    WHEN YOUR MODEL AND THE ORIGINAL MACHINE CODE DISAGREE,
    THE MACHINE CODE IS RIGHT -- INCLUDING WHEN IT LOOKS WRONG.

Everything else in this manual is machinery for making that rule
enforceable. Three corollaries you must internalise:

  1.1 EVERY RULE YOU WRITE IS A HYPOTHESIS UNTIL THE ORIGINAL HAS ANSWERED
      FOR IT. Not until it looks right, not until it plays right, not until
      your test passes -- until the original was made to answer the same
      question and gave the same answer. This is why the verification
      harness is built BEFORE the engine (phase 7 before phase 12): a
      standard you cannot enforce is a standard you do not have.

  1.2 WHEN A ROUTINE LOOKS LIKE IT IS CUTTING A CORNER, FIND OUT WHAT
      COMPENSATES BEFORE YOU FIX IT. This is the single highest-value habit
      in the document. Eight-bit games are dense with compensations that
      live somewhere other than the code you are reading -- in the artwork,
      in a table, in the level data, in a wrap that only happens on one
      value. The archetype is section 4, F1: read it.

  1.3 THE QUIRKS ARE THE GAME. A skipped bounds check, a one-pixel
      asymmetry, a hesitation, a state where the player stands on nothing:
      these are not defects to clean up, they are what the original felt
      like. Prove each one against the original, then FREEZE IT IN A
      REGRESSION TEST carrying the original's own answer, because a quirk
      without a test will eventually be removed by someone acting in good
      faith -- possibly you, three days later.

And the meta-rule that governs your own reporting: never quote a claim at
higher confidence than the evidence supports (section 5). "It looks right"
is not evidence. "The differential test reports 900/900" is not evidence
either, unless you also printed the branch histogram.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE PIPELINE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Sixteen phases in four movements:

    Phases 1-6    STATIC EXTRACTION     (bytes off the tape, decoded)
    Phase  7      THE PIVOT             (a working Z80 simulator harness)
    Phases 8-11   MEASUREMENT           (asking the original questions)
    Phase  12     CONSTRUCTION          (the only phase that writes engine)
    Phases 13-16  PROOF AND RECORD

Two ordering rules, and they are not in tension even though they sound it:

  STATIC EXTRACTION BEFORE THE SIMULATOR. The simulator needs the extracted
  binary to load. That is phases 1-2, not phases 5-6.

  THE SIMULATOR BEFORE THE ENGINE. Not one line of gameplay logic gets
  written before you can ask the original Z80 whether it is right.

THE ONE ORDERING LOOP, AND IT IS DELIBERATE. Phases 5 and 6 both have a
cheap version that runs before the harness and a strong version that runs
after it.

  - Phase 5 without a reference disassembly cannot know which byte ranges
    are graphics or where each block starts, because dimensions come from
    draw routines (phase 10) and code/data separation comes from execution
    coverage (phase 8). So phase 5 SPLITS: a crude contact-sheet sweep of
    the whole binary now, real extraction after phase 10.
  - Phase 6 needs a ground-truth frame. The strong route is to dump
    $4000-$5AFF out of your own harness at a live level, which needs
    phases 7-8. Run phase 6 then.

The numbering is a dependency order, not a calendar. Do the cheap version
first, come back with the harness, and do it properly.

Every phase has an EXIT GATE that is an observable number or a printed
artifact, not a feeling. If you cannot state the gate as something you
could put in a log line, you have not finished the phase. Good gates look
like: "bytes compared 9748 / mismatching 0 / skipped 69"; "672/672 buffer
bytes identical"; "900/900 transitions with every branch exercised at
least once"; "35.8% versus 96.3%, a 2.7x separation"; "99.44% of pixels,
35 of 672 cells differing, all 35 attributed in writing". A gate that
reports 99% with an unattributed remainder is not a gate.


PHASE 1 -- READ THE TAPE
........................

INPUT: the .tzx or .tap file.
OUTPUT: one binary blob per data block, plus a manifest of block types and
        lengths.
TOOLING: write your own parser against the TZX spec (0.6). You need to
        control it, and you need it to stop rather than guess.

  - TAP is trivial: repeating [2-byte length][flag][data][XOR checksum].
    TZX is a container: a 10-byte header ("ZXTape!" 0x1A, major, minor)
    then ID-tagged blocks.
  - Handle, at minimum, the blocks that carry data: 0x10 (standard speed),
    0x11 (turbo), 0x14 (pure data). All three carry an explicit data-length
    field and decode with the same code; only the pulse timings differ, and
    the timings do not matter to you because you are not reproducing the
    load. Also handle 0x12 (pure tone), 0x13 (pulse sequence), 0x20
    (pause), 0x30 (text), 0x32 (archive info).
  - Handle the ones that make a parser give a WRONG answer rather than an
    honest stop, because they restructure the block sequence: 0x24/0x25
    (loop start/end -- ignoring them emits the wrong NUMBER of blocks,
    silently), 0x21/0x22 (group start/end), 0x28 (select), 0x2A (stop the
    tape if 48K), 0x33 (hardware type).
  - The ones that genuinely defeat a block parser are 0x15 (direct
    recording), 0x18 (CSW) and 0x19 (generalized data). If you see those,
    or a payload the loader decrypts in RAM, you are on the snapshot route
    (section 8, Q1).
  - PRINT AND STOP on any ID you do not handle. Never skip. A skipped
    0x12/0x13 pair is usually the pilot for a following 0x14, and treating
    that 0x14 as freestanding gives you a byte-shifted image that
    disassembles into plausible nonsense -- the worst possible failure,
    because it looks like progress.
  - Verify every checksum: XOR-fold over the flag byte and the body, compare
    to the last byte. One bad checksum invalidates everything downstream.
  - Standard headers (flag 0x00, 19 bytes) give you type, filename, length
    and two type-dependent parameters. For a BASIC header, param2 is the
    "variables area" offset. If it is far smaller than the block length,
    only the first N bytes are real BASIC and the rest is binary smuggled
    in as fake variables. In the case study the header reads
    len=1624, vars@18 -- eighteen bytes of BASIC and 1,606 bytes of
    loader, character set and title screen riding along behind it.
    THE OTHER READING IS ALSO COMMON: a genuinely large variables area,
    because 1982-84 games routinely store level data in DIM'd string
    arrays. Detokenise and look before concluding.
  - WRITE A BASIC DETOKENISER. It is 100 lines (tokens are byte values
    0xA5-0xFF; numbers carry a 5-byte binary form after the digits, which
    you skip). For a substantially-BASIC game it IS the disassembly.
  - Headerless blocks (flag 0xFF) carry no destination address. It is not
    on the tape. It is in the loader. See phase 2.

GATE: every data block reports a clean checksum; no unhandled block ID; the
length in each header equals the length of the block that follows it; you
can state what each block is for, including the small ones.

CASE STUDY: 20,611 bytes, TZX v1.10, one 0x30 text block ("Created with
Ramsoft MakeTZX") and four standard-speed 0x10 blocks -- a 19-byte BASIC
header, a 1,624-byte payload, a TWO-byte block, and 18,900 bytes of game.
All checksums clean. Nothing to defeat. THIS IS THE EASIEST POSSIBLE CASE
and you must not assume it. See section 8, Q1.


PHASE 2 -- THE LOADER AND THE MEMORY MAP
.........................................

INPUT: the decoded blocks.
OUTPUT: a table of (block -> load address, length), the game's entry point,
        and a list of MACHINE STATE the loader leaves behind.
TOOLING: a Z80 disassembler you drive yourself.

  - The BASIC line tells you where the loader starts: RANDOMIZE USR nnnnn.
    Convert and disassemble from there.
  - Each ROM tape load is CALL $0556 (LD-BYTES). At each call site read the
    registers set immediately before it: IX = destination, DE = length,
    A = expected flag ($FF headerless, $00 header), carry set = load rather
    than verify. Each call site is one headerless block in tape order. THAT
    LIST IS YOUR MEMORY MAP. The JP at the end of the loader is your entry
    point. In the case study the last three steps read:
        LD IX,$8214 / LD DE,$49D4 / SCF / LD A,$FF / CALL $0556
        LD HL,$5DC0 / LD ($5C63),HL / LD ($5C65),HL
        JP $A410
    -- 18,900 bytes to $8214, STKBOT and STKEND pointed at $5DC0, entry at
    $A410.
  - A custom loader that does its own edge detection instead of calling
    $0556 means you are on the harder path -- section 8, Q1.
  - THE LOADER'S SIDE EFFECTS ARE PART OF THE MEMORY IMAGE. You need every
    write below $5C00 and every write into the system variables at
    $5C00-$5CB5. Two ways to get them, and do both:
      (a) READ THE LOADER BY EYE. It is usually under a hundred
          instructions and every write is visible. This is what the case
          study did; the loader reduced to a nine-step flow.
      (b) CONFIRM DYNAMICALLY, ONCE. Your simulator library probably does
          not give you a memory-write hook -- SkoolKit's tracer interface
          exposes read_port and write_port only. Either subclass the
          memory object (fastest: wrap __setitem__ and record
          (PC, address, value)), or snapshot $0000-$5CCB before and after
          and diff. The subclass costs perhaps 2x on execution speed and
          is worth it for one run. Run the loader ONCE, in a full emulator
          or with a real ROM image, purely to enumerate. Then never run it
          again -- section 6.1 builds the image directly.
  - Small blocks are not noise. In the case study a TWO-BYTE block loads
    $0001 into $5C3D (ERR_SP). The game's main loop opens with
    LD HL,($5C3D) / DEC HL / LD A,H / OR L / JR nz,$985E -- where $985E is
    the address of the JR itself and nothing in the loop changes the flags.
    It is a one-shot guard that passes exactly once, only because the
    loader wrote that value. $5C3D is read once in the whole game and never
    written. Omit it from your memory image and the game hangs on its first
    instruction, forever, in a two-byte infinite loop.

GATE: every headerless block is accounted for by a load call with a
matching length; the final jump lands inside a block you loaded; you can
draw the whole 64K map with every region attributed; the list of
loader-set state is written down.

INDEPENDENT CONFIRMATION IS CHEAP HERE AND WORTH IT. In the case study, the
inferred boundary between the custom character set and the loader code was
confirmed by counting the title screen: 768 cells, 568 of them ROM-font
characters and 200 custom, codes 32-161, giving custom indices 0-65 --
exactly the 66 tile definitions sitting at $5CE3, which is why the tile
data ends precisely where the code begins at $5EF3. Two derivations
agreeing beats one derivation. Look for the second one.


PHASE 3 -- FIND AND VERIFY ANY THIRD-PARTY DISASSEMBLY
.......................................................

INPUT: your extracted game block.
OUTPUT: either a verified reference you may lean on, or a decision to
        proceed without one.

  - Search for an existing disassembly of your game. For popular titles a
    SkoolKit project or a commented .asm may exist. It can save you days.
  - DO NOT TRUST ONE LABEL OF IT UNTIL YOU HAVE VERIFIED IT BYTE-FOR-BYTE
    AGAINST YOUR OWN EXTRACTION. Write a 50-line checker: pull every data
    byte declaration out of the reference, parse hex and decimal forms, map
    through its stated ORG, and compare with your bytes. Report THREE
    numbers: compared, mismatching, skipped. A checker that silently skips
    lines it cannot parse and then reports zero mismatches is lying to you.
    The case study's checker prints:
        DEFB bytes compared : 9748
        mismatching lines   : 0
        skipped lines       : 69  (strings/expressions/out-of-range)
    69 is a legitimate skip category BECAUSE IT IS NAMED. "Skipped 69" with
    no reason is a hole.
  - Then write down an honest COVERAGE assessment before relying on it. In
    the case study the reference was excellent at the data layer -- 174
    byte blocks, 16 word blocks, 17 text blocks typed, all 8 levels
    located, every graphic dimensioned, 152 labels, 96 routines titled --
    but only about 10% of instructions carried comments (366 of 3,536
    lines). So the physics routines were TITLED and their bodies BARE. That
    is the honest statement of where the remaining work is, and it is
    useful precisely because it is unflattering.
  - Titles are readings, not derivations. In the case study two were wrong
    and both mattered: $9E66, titled "checks if there's enough headroom
    for a jump", is the farmer/entity overlap test (a 13-wide, 28-tall
    box); $734A/$734B, marked "Unknown variable", are the duck's X and Y
    velocities, signed and clamped to plus or minus 5.
  - VALIDATE YOUR DECODE AGAINST DOCUMENTED BEHAVIOUR, AND EXPECT TO WIN
    SOMETIMES. The most reproducible instance in the case study: the
    control tables. Three 12-byte tables at $8250, $825C, $8268, each six
    (mask, half-row-select) words in UP/DOWN/LEFT/RIGHT/JUMP/ALT order.
    Decoding them by hand gives 2,W,9,0,Z,M; then 7,6,5,8,4,9; then
    Q,A,O,P,M,1. The reference's comments read "2, W, 9, 0, Z or M" and
    "Q, A, O, P, M or 1" -- exact, which validates the decode -- and
    "Cursor Keys, 4 or S" for the middle one. Cursor keys (5,6,7,8) and 4
    are right; the alternate jump is 9, not S. The game's own on-screen
    text at $8410 reads "2     --cursor--keys--    4 or 9". Two agreeing
    sources beat the reference.
  - Feed corrections back upstream. Also answer the reference's own open
    questions if you can -- in the case study its unanswered "loads two
    bytes, to which address?" was answered straight out of the loader
    ($5FA2: LD IX,$5C3D / LD DE,$0002 / SCF / LD A,$FF / CALL $0556).

GATE: a printed line of the form "bytes compared N / mismatching 0 /
skipped M", with M enumerated and explained. If no reference exists, an
explicit note saying so and a plan for the extra work (section 8, Q6).


PHASE 4 -- BUILD AN ANNOTATED DISASSEMBLER
...........................................

INPUT: the game block plus everything you have learned.
OUTPUT: a tool you will run hundreds of times.

Do not read raw disassembly for a week. Spend two hours writing a wrapper
that:

  - decodes with a real Z80 decoder (z80dis, or your library's own), not a
    hand-rolled table;
  - substitutes symbolic names into operands by regex over 3-4 digit hex
    addresses, so you read "LD HL,level_buffer" and not "LD HL,$61A8";
  - prints the block description above each region;
  - prints raw hex bytes beside each instruction (you will need it for
    false-positive checks and for verifying instruction boundaries);
  - keeps an EXTRA dictionary of names you invented yourself, which grows
    as you learn. In the case study this accumulated entries like
    farmer_frame_ptr, farmer_jump_phase, farmer_air_x, level_buffer.

The difference between reading LD HL,$61A8 and LD HL,level_buffer is the
difference between transcribing and understanding. Every routine you will
have to reason about in phase 10 gets read through this tool.

GATE: you can print any address range with labels resolved, and adding a
new name takes one line.


PHASE 5 -- STATIC EXTRACTION OF DATA
....................................

INPUT: game block, plus block typing from phase 3 if you have it.
OUTPUT: level maps, sprite and tile bitmaps, spawn tables, text, all in a
        machine-readable manifest.

WITH A REFERENCE this is a day. WITHOUT ONE IT SPLITS IN TWO, and you must
plan for that:

  5.0a NOW: a crude contact-sheet sweep. Render the whole binary as
       8-pixel-wide monochrome, cell-major and row-major, at several
       column widths, into a few large PNGs, and LOOK. Graphics regions
       are unmistakable to the eye and invisible to statistics. Mark the
       ranges. That is all you can honestly do yet.
  5.0b AFTER PHASE 10: real extraction. Dimensions come out of each draw
       routine's inner loop; code/data separation comes out of execution
       coverage (section 8, Q13) plus recursive descent from the entry
       point; and TILE SEMANTICS -- which id means floor -- come out of
       the PREDICATES, not the artwork. The closure test proves the format
       closes; it never tells you what a tile means. In the case study
       "floor" is defined by the support test's CP 5 at $B34C and the
       landing routine's ranges at $A356, both phase 10 work.

Three decisions here silently corrupt everything downstream if you get
them wrong. None of them announces itself.

  5.1 BYTE LAYOUT. Spectrum games routinely use two bitmap layouts in one
      binary:
        - CELL-MAJOR: each 8x8 character cell is 8 consecutive bytes. Used
          by tile engines, fonts, UI. If a reference gives you
          "#UDGARRAY<n>;<start>-<end>-8" macros, the step of 8 IS the
          statement that this block is cell-major.
        - ROW-MAJOR: (cell-width) bytes per pixel row, interleaved across
          cells. Used by sprite blitters that draw a full 16-pixel row at a
          time.
      THE NUMBER OF LAYOUTS EQUALS THE NUMBER OF DRAW ROUTINES. Do not
      reason about which is which. Render every candidate block BOTH WAYS
      into one contact sheet PNG and look at it. In the case study a
      birdcage came out as noise and the farmer as a smear until this was
      done. Then tag every block with an explicit layout field in the
      manifest so that no downstream consumer ever has to guess.
  5.2 DIMENSIONS. Get them from a source, not a guess. In order of
      preference: a reference's declared dimensions; a "#UDGARRAY" macro;
      the DRAW ROUTINE'S OWN INNER LOOP. That last one always works and
      needs no reference at all -- in the case study the ostrich draw
      routine walks 32 bytes backwards two at a time, which is 2 bytes wide
      by 16 rows, which is where "16x16" comes from.
  5.3 TABLES ARE FOUND BY READING THEIR LOADERS, NOT BY PATTERN HUNTING.
      Find the code that copies a table into working RAM and read it. It
      tells you the stride, the index arithmetic, what a leading count byte
      means, which slots are pre-cleared to a sentinel, and -- critically
      -- which fields are DERIVED rather than stored. In the case study the
      ostrich table has a 21-byte stride indexed by (level+1), holding
      [count][5 x (x, y, state, spare)], with slots pre-cleared to $FF; and
      the second lift is not in any table at all, its y hard-coded and its
      state word computed as lift 1's minus $0800 -- exactly one display
      third, which is the 64-pixel separation you can see on screen.

GATE: a CLOSURE TEST. Decode with ZERO unrecognised values, and reproduce
an invariant you knew independently. In the case study: 8 levels at base
$B3B0, stride $2A0 (672 = 32 x 21 tiles), every level decoding with no
unknown tile ids, and every level containing exactly 12 eggs -- which is
the game's own rule, stated in its manual. (Corn is also collectible and
does NOT follow a rule: counts are 10, 7, 10, 6, 13, 10, 4, 38. Pick the
invariant that is actually invariant.) If 10-15% of your data is
unclassified, you have the WRONG format, not a slightly wrong one. If your
game has no such invariant, say so and lean harder on phase 6 and phase 8.


PHASE 6 -- GROUND TRUTH FROM A REAL FRAME
..........................................

OUTPUT: settled answers to three questions the binary alone will not
        settle, plus a reusable pixel-diff tool.

INPUT -- AND THIS IS THE PART THAT IS USUALLY LEFT VAGUE. You need at
least one frame of the original running, at a level you can identify. Two
routes, and the first is strictly better:

  ROUTE A -- OUT OF YOUR OWN HARNESS (needs phases 7-8; preferred).
    Boot to a live level, then dump the display file $4000-$57FF (6,144
    bytes) and the attribute file $5800-$5AFF (768 bytes) straight out of
    simulator memory and de-interleave them with the formula in 3.2. You
    get a perfect native 256x192 frame with known pixel values and known
    attributes. NO SCALE SEARCH, NO GRID FIT, NO COLOUR CLASSIFIER, no
    human, and you can reach any level by poking the level counter. Do
    this. It is thirty lines and it removes three whole error classes.
  ROUTE B -- AN EMULATOR CAPTURE (what the case study actually did).
    Load the .tzx in Fuse or ZEsarUX, play to the level, screenshot. Fix
    the capture parameters and write them down: integer scale (2x or 3x),
    border included or excluded, PNG, no filters, no scanline emulation.
    You will then need the grid fit below, because a scaled screenshot's
    cell size is fractional -- the case study's fit came out at
    x0=41.0 y0=32.5 cell=33.1875 x 33.20, which no one measures by hand.
    If the game ships an RZX recording, replay it: it is a source of
    frames AND a substitute play-tester.

  6.1 ROW ORDER / AXIS DIRECTION. Do not read the draw routine and decide.
      DECODE YOUR LEVEL DATA BOTH WAYS AND SCORE BOTH AGAINST ONE REAL
      FRAME. In the case study, top-down scored 78 of 218 classified cells
      (35.8%) and bottom-up scored 210 of 218 (96.3%). The 2.7x separation
      is the gate; a 55-versus-52 would have proved nothing and would have
      meant your classifier was broken. THEN go and find the address
      routine and confirm WHY -- in the case study it inverts twice, once
      for the character row and once for the pixel row, and the level data
      is stored bottom-up because the game's entire y axis is bottom-up
      (screen_y = 191 - game_y), with the buffer filled by a plain block
      copy and no flipping anywhere.
  6.2 PALETTE. Census the inks in the frame per object type. Then close it
      out against the attribute-writing code. In the case study the colour
      is not in the level data at all: at $9555 the attribute refresh does
      CP 9 / JR c to a table at $984F, so the table is indexed by tile ids
      0-8 -- NINE entries, holding 06 03 03 07 03 04 06 07 07 -- with ids
      >= 9 (the birdcage) hard-coded to attr 6. Every value is below 64,
      WHICH MEANS THE BRIGHT BIT IS NEVER SET. Render at 0xD7 components,
      not 0xFF. A real frame samples the green floor at RGB(0,200,0) --
      that confirms "not bright"; it does not by itself pin 0xD7 (=215)
      against any other non-bright rendering. Reaching for the vivid
      palette by default is wrong for any such game and is VISIBLY wrong.
  6.3 GEOMETRY (route B only). Establish the pixel grid by landmark plus a
      small brute force, and PRINT the search. Do not hand-measure an
      origin and trust it. In the case study the two red HUD bands are
      exactly two character rows apart, which anchors the vertical fit;
      the horizontal fit is a search over dx and cell width.

TWO TECHNIQUES WORTH STEALING WHOLESALE:

  - TEMPLATE-MATCH WITH THE GAME'S OWN EXTRACTED BITMAPS, scoring only over
    the sprite's set pixels (score = 3*hits - 2*total works well; because
    total is constant per template the whole search vectorises into one
    sliding window). This finds entities that colour-anomaly detection
    cannot see. In the case study the player was invisible to anomaly
    detection on one level because he spawns in front of a same-coloured
    ladder; template matching found him on all eight at full confidence.
  - READ HUD TEXT WITH THE GAME'S OWN EXTRACTED FONT. It is exact where OCR
    is fuzzy. This is how a scoring formula was verified across nine
    levels.

WARNING, AND IT IS A BIG ONE: SCREENSHOTS FALSIFY, TABLES DEFINE. A "level
start" capture is already several frames into the level. In the case study
the screenshots said one level had three enemies where the table says four,
and another had two where the table says three; every lift measured
consistently 6 pixels risen because lifts start moving immediately; and an
entity's position read 3 pixels off because the capture came from a later
level where it had already been released. Anything the game TABULATES beats
anything you measured from a frame. Keep the screenshot-derived section in
your notes, marked "superseded", so the correction stays traceable.

GATE: 99%+ of play-area pixels agree AND EVERY DIFFERING CELL IS
ATTRIBUTED IN WRITING. Be precise about what that means, because the case
study did NOT close it to zero by instrument. Its tool prints:

    99.44% play-area pixels agree
    cells differing : 35 of 672
    cells differing outside known entity positions: 25

and the 25 -- two birdcage cells at 29 and 23 px, a corn cell at 10 px, a
dozen 1-3 px floor cells at the right edge -- are accounted for in the
NOTES by argument (a caged entity, an uncaptured sprite, right-edge
resampling), not by the tool. That is an acceptable pass. "99% and I don't
know why the rest differ" is not.

IF YOUR GAME HAS NO STATIC TILE LAYER -- a scroller, an object-list room, a
game whose motion lives entirely in the attribute file -- this gate does
not apply as written. See 3.11 and section 8, Q15.

PHASE 8 -- DRIVE INTO LIVE PLAY, AND VALIDATE STATIC AGAINST LIVE
..................................................................

INPUT: the harness.
OUTPUT: a reusable function that boots the game to a live level, plus proof
        that your static extraction was right about the right bytes.

  8.1 FIND THE MENU KEYS FROM THE BINARY. Do not read the instructions
      screen and do not brute-force 40 key bits. Run with no keys pressed
      and single-step to the first port read whose LOW byte is $FE. Log
      the HIGH byte (that is the half-row select) and then read the next
      few instructions to see which bit the game tests. That pair is the
      key. In the case study this yields, in order:
        $A4AB tests BIT 1 of the value read from port $FDFE  -> "S" starts
        $A5D3 waits reading port $F7FE for bits 0-3          -> "1".."4"
              players
      Two lines of scripting, derived, not guessed. The same trick finds
      the in-game controls, but prefer the game's own control table (3.4)
      because the keys are redefinable.
  8.2 FIND THE WORKING-RAM STRUCTURES. This is the technique that replaces
      every label a reference would have given you, and it is three lines:
      SCAN ALL 64K FOR A WINDOW MATCHING BYTES YOU ALREADY DECODED. Every
      few thousand instructions, search memory for your decoded level's
      first 32 bytes; report the address it lands at. That address is the
      live level buffer. In the case study it is $61A8, which the harness
      then hard-codes.
      For variables rather than buffers, DIFF MEMORY ACROSS A KNOWN
      TRANSITION: snapshot, do one thing (press right for 200 passes; kill
      the player; collect an item), snapshot again, and list every changed
      byte. A handful of addresses change per transition and they are your
      variable map. This is how you find entity slots, the round-robin
      index and the random-source pointer without a reference -- in the
      case study $7357, $7356 and $736C.
  8.3 SCRIPT THE MENUS the way a player would, pressing keys the way
      section 6.5 says, or you will get precise nonsense.
  8.4 DO NOT guess how many instructions setup takes. SYNCHRONISE ON DATA
      YOU ALREADY EXTRACTED: single-step, and every couple of thousand
      instructions compare the first 32 bytes of the live level buffer
      against your statically decoded level. Stop when they match. This
      gives you an instruction count that regressions will change, and it
      synchronises on meaning rather than on timing.
  8.5 Then compare EVERYTHING: live entity slots against your spawn tables,
      the full level buffer against your decoded map, HUD values against
      your formulas.

GATE: "ALL CHECKS PASSED", including a line of the form "level buffer
versus decoded level N: 672/672 IDENTICAL". This is the moment your static
extraction stops being a hypothesis. Static extraction can be perfectly
right about the bytes and wrong about WHICH bytes; only this catches that.


PHASE 9 -- MEASURE TIME
.......................

INPUT: a live level in the harness.
OUTPUT: the game's clock model, in T-states.

  - Measure the cost of one main-loop pass by stepping off the loop entry
    and running back to it, diffing the T-state counter. Do it thousands of
    times.
  - PASS COST IS NOT CONSTANT, AND YOU DO NOT YET KNOW WHICH STATES MATTER.
    So do not start by measuring "each state" -- you do not have the list.
    HISTOGRAM THE PER-PASS COST OVER TENS OF THOUSANDS OF PASSES AND LOOK
    FOR MULTI-MODALITY. Each mode is a branch you have not identified. Then
    go and find what distinguishes them, and only then measure each
    separately. In the case study the modes are grounded 406 T-states and
    airborne 560 -- 1.38x, because the airborne path runs the whole
    $A21C/$A2B5 chain every pass. THE GAME LITERALLY SLOWS DOWN WHILE THE
    PLAYER IS IN THE AIR. A fixed pass rate makes falls about 1.4x too
    fast and NO GRAVITY CONSTANT WILL FIX IT, because the error is in the
    clock and not the physics.
  - Measure derived rates the same way: pixels per second per entity, ticks
    per displayed timer unit, jump duration, animation cadence.
  - THREE MEASUREMENT TRAPS, all of which were hit for real:
      (a) ACCUMULATE DISTANCE, NEVER END-MINUS-START. A patrolling entity
          oscillates; sampled over 13.6 seconds one went
          104-112-96-84-80-64-52-68-84-100-116 and an early run duly
          reported "0 px/sec". When a measurement returns exactly zero,
          suspect the measurement.
      (b) A CLEAN DERIVATION CAN STILL BE WRONG. "4 pixels per 5 long
          passes" predicts 54 px/sec; the real answer is 17.3, because of a
          divider you had not noticed. Measure first, then explain the
          measurement.
      (c) SOMETIMES MEASUREMENT WINS WITH NO EXPLANATION. In the case study
          the timer chain read from code predicts 6,500 passes per
          displayed unit; measurement in a live level gives 1,181; only the
          measurement produces the two-minute level the original has. The
          engine uses 1,181, and the discrepancy is logged as OPEN, in the
          notes and in a comment at the constant. NEVER quietly split the
          difference and never adjust the measurement to match your
          derivation.
  - TWO DERIVATIONS AGREEING CLOSES A QUESTION -- BUT ONLY IF THEY ARE
    INDEPENDENT. In the case study, walking speed came out at 67.6 px/sec
    from a live run (99 pixels moved over 12,870 passes) and from
    8,783 passes/sec divided by the 130-pass housekeeping interval. Those
    two share the measured pass rate, so their agreement confirms the
    divider is 130 and nothing about the absolute rate. THE INDEPENDENT
    CHECKS ARE THE HUMAN-SCALE ONES: 3.8 seconds to cross the screen, a
    two-minute level clock, a 0.64-second jump, and an 11-pixel rise which,
    COMBINED WITH the 8-pixel landing snap, just reaches a platform one
    character cell higher (a cell is 8 pixels; the snap is what closes the
    remaining 3). Those catch an error of 130x. Internal consistency never
    will.

GATE: every headline timing figure cross-checked two ways, at least one of
which is external/human-scale; every unexplained disagreement written down
as open.


PHASE 10 -- READ THE ROUTINES AND WRITE THE BEHAVIOURAL SPEC
.............................................................

INPUT: the annotated disassembler, the harness, and time.
OUTPUT: prose that states every rule, with the address it came from.

This is the phase no tool can do for you, and it is where the game
actually lives. Recover things in this order:

  10.1 THE LOOP SHAPE. What is the top of the main loop? What runs every
       pass? What runs only occasionally? In the case study the common path
       is about 15 instructions, and a LONG PATH -- keys, timers, enemy
       updates, player horizontal movement -- runs only when a counter
       wraps, once per 130 passes.
  10.2 THE DIVIDER CHAIN on top of that. THIS IS THE CANONICAL STATEMENT;
       everything else in the manual points here. Per-entity counters gate
       updates further. In the case study, three layers:
         (1) frame counter $72DC counts down from $82 (130): one long path
             per 130 passes;
         (2) per-entity dividers on top of that -- lifts every 2 long
             passes, a bonus tick every 10, the duck every 12, ostriches
             every 3 via $736B, or every 2 once cleared_levels reaches
             $20, WHICH IS LITERALLY THE DOCUMENTED "on pass 5 the birds
             get faster" RULE, exactly 1.5x;
         (3) ostriches update ROUND-ROBIN across five slots, empty slots
             returning early but still consuming their turn, so enemy speed
             does not depend on how many are alive.
       Net: one ostrich moves once per 130 x 3 x 5 = 1,950 passes. Reading
       "one pixel per iteration" literally over-states walking by 130x and
       enemies by 1,950x. WRITE THE DIVIDER CHAIN OUT AS A TABLE, then
       measure, then check the two agree.
  10.3 THE COORDINATE SYSTEM, per draw routine, not per game.
  10.4 THE TILE LOOKUP, arithmetically and exactly, including any masking,
       because rules elsewhere will depend on the wrap. In the case study
       $9E34 is level_buffer + (y & $F8)*4 + (x >> 3) with x and y masked
       to 8 bits, and the masking is the register width, not defensive
       coding.
  10.5 THEN EACH RULE, WITH ITS QUIRKS INTACT.

FIVE Z80 IDIOMS THAT MUST SURVIVE TRANSLATION. Each cost a real bug in the
case study; each is expanded in section 4, family C:

  (a) STACK UNWINDING AS CONTROL FLOW. Extra POPs before a RET discard a
      caller's return address. This is how "abandon the rest of this
      update", "the player dies", "level complete" and "item collected" are
      signalled. It reads as dead code when you are skimming for
      arithmetic. Two entire death conditions were missing from the engine
      because of this.
  (b) BYTE WRAPPING IS LOAD-BEARING. DEC on 0 gives 255, so a counter
      initialised to 0 means a full 256-iteration wait, and the original
      visibly hangs at exactly those moments.
  (c) FALL-THROUGH THAT LOOKS LIKE A BUG. A conditional store followed by
      an UNCONDITIONAL increment changes the stored value. Adding an early
      return "for clarity" froze the standing player mid-stride.
  (d) GATES THAT SKIP THE CHECK ENTIRELY. "AND 7 / RET z" means the check
      simply does not happen on aligned positions, and that produces
      exactly one pixel of overhang per platform edge -- which is the feel
      of the game.
  (e) TRANSIENT STATES AND EMPTY DISPATCH ARMS. A three-way dispatch whose
      third case does NOTHING AT ALL, for four long passes, is not a
      no-op -- it is a DURATION, and the player sees it as a hesitation at
      the lip of a platform.

WHEN THE CODE WILL NOT ANSWER, ASK THE SIMULATOR. Construct the scenario by
poking state and observe: put the player in free fall by writing his
position, airborne flag, velocity and phase, then record T-states per pixel
of movement. Drop an enemy onto him and single-step to the death routine to
see what survives. Place him at every x across a platform edge and
enumerate the outcomes. This turns "what does the game do here" into an
experiment. Recipes in 6.8.

ALSO: RECORD YOUR CORRECTIONS to any third-party source as you find them.

GATE: a written spec in which every rule cites an address, and every number
is either measured or read from a specific instruction.


PHASE 11 -- SOUND
.................

INPUT: the harness.
OUTPUT: a parameter model for every effect, verified by measurement, AND a
        scheduling bridge into the browser.

  11.1 FIND THE SPEAKER WRITES -- ALL THE FORMS. It is NOT enough to grep
       for D3 FE. The port is $FE and bit 4 is the speaker, but the write
       can be any of:
           D3 FE           OUT ($FE),A
           ED 79           OUT (C),A     with BC = $xxFE
           ED 41/49/51/...  OUT (C),r     likewise
       plus table-driven and self-modified port writes. A reader who greps
       one form and finds nothing concludes "this binary has no speaker
       writes" and abandons a subsystem. DO BOTH: grep every form, AND run
       the harness with a port-write tracer over a few million instructions
       of driven play, recording every write whose LOW byte is $FE together
       with its T-stamp and the PC. The dynamic scan cannot miss.
  11.2 CONFIRM EACH STATIC HIT against a disassembly walked from a known
       instruction boundary -- try start offsets 2 to 40 bytes back,
       disassemble forward, and keep the first window whose instruction
       boundaries land exactly on the candidate. In the case study there
       were exactly two speaker writes in 18,900 bytes and both were in one
       routine ($9CA4); a third byte-pattern hit was a relative jump's
       displacement byte followed by the next opcode.
  11.3 DECIDE THE ARCHITECTURE BY MEASUREMENT, NOT BY ASSUMPTION. Beeper
       pitch is set by the interval between speaker writes, so whoever
       produces those writes owns the CPU for that interval -- but that
       does NOT mean the game stops. Histogram the T-state gap between the
       first and last speaker write in each 69,888-T window and see which
       of these you have:
         (a) ONE BLOCKING ROUTINE. A note runs to completion and the game
             is frozen for its duration. This is the case study, and it is
             what makes 11.4's parameter fit valid.
         (b) A PER-FRAME SLICE. The ISR or main loop plays a fixed T-state
             chunk of tone each frame and the game runs the rest. This is
             how most in-game beeper MUSIC is done. Model the slice, not
             the note.
         (c) A MULTI-CHANNEL ENGINE (Follin / Free Radical style) that
             interleaves two or three pitch counters in one loop. It
             blocks, but the output is a superposed pulse train and a
             "fit high = 13H+14" model collapses entirely. You must
             REPRODUCE THE COUNTER ALGORITHM as code and assert against
             the recorded edge train.
         (d) ONE TOGGLE PER ISR, where pitch is a function of a frame
             divider and nothing blocks.
       Get this wrong in direction (a) and your port stops the world on
       every note.
  11.4 FOR CASE (a), DERIVE THE WAVEFORM MODEL BY INSTRUMENTED SIMULATION,
       NEVER BY HAND COUNTING. Call the routine in isolation with its
       registers set (6.6), with a tracer on port writes recording
       (T-state, level) on every change of the speaker bit, and fit the
       model across the useful parameter range. In the case study the
       eleven-instruction routine at $9CA4 gives high = 13H+14, low =
       13H+33 (the loop overhead all falls on the low half), period =
       26H+47 -- and H=0 MEANS 256 because DJNZ with B=0 wraps. Those two
       facts, the asymmetry and the wrap, are exactly where a hand count
       goes wrong.
  11.5 CHECK A RANGE, NOT A POINT, and assert the EDGE COUNT as well as the
       periods -- an edge count catches an off-by-one that period lengths
       do not. Then make the check a BUILD GATE that refuses to emit the
       sound asset if the model disagrees with the trace.
  11.6 WORK OUT WHAT GATES EACH EFFECT: position, time, or a counter.
       Position-gated sound is locked to movement speed by construction; in
       the case study footsteps ($9D60) and ladder rungs ($9EDE) are
       triggered on "coordinate AND 3", so cadence and walking speed cannot
       drift apart.
  11.7 EXPECT AT LEAST ONE EFFECT WHOSE PARAMETER IS A LIVE GAME VALUE.
       This is a different modelling problem from a table of constants and
       it is easy to miss. In the case study $9884 plays a single cycle
       with H = 255 - y once per frame-counter wrap while airborne: the y
       axis is bottom-up, so as the player falls H rises, the period
       lengthens and THE PITCH FALLS WITH HIM. It is not a tone, it is a
       pulse train whose pulse width tracks his height.
  11.8 TUNES ARE USUALLY A DIFFERENT MECHANISM ENTIRELY. In the case study
       $AB60 walks byte pairs and feeds them to the ROM BEEP entry point
       ($2D28 STACK-A, RST $28 to divide by ten, CALL $03F8), so each pair
       is (duration in tenths of a second, semitones above middle C), pitch
       0 being middle C. ROM BEEP BLOCKS, and $A6FE calls it before doing
       anything else, so the original genuinely freezes for 4.8 seconds on
       every death. Reproduce that. It is the game.
  11.9 READING A FORMULA IS NOT RUNNING IT. In the case study the pickup
       warble at $9860 loads sfx_timer with $FF and plays when the
       pre-decrement value has (v & 3) == 0, at H = ((v-1) & 31) + 6. Read
       alone, that is a smooth 32-step sweep. Under the & 3 gate the
       expression can produce exactly EIGHT values -- 37, 33, 29, 25, 21,
       17, 13, 9 -- a descending arpeggio run eight times, 63 blips in
       about 0.6 seconds. A test that generated the sequence independently
       and compared pitch SETS disagreed with the prose; the test was right
       and the implementation had been correct all along. ENUMERATE THE
       REACHABLE OUTPUTS OF ANY FORMULA UNDER ITS GUARD. Do not read the
       algebra.
  11.10 THE BRIDGE INTO THE BROWSER, which is the step everyone invents
       badly. Your engine drains a whole animation frame's worth of passes
       in one burst, so playing each blip on arrival stacks a frame of
       footsteps into a single instant. EVERY SOUND EVENT MUST CARRY THE
       SIMULATED T-STATE COUNT AT WHICH IT HAPPENED, and the player
       converts that to an AudioContext time, resyncing only when the two
       have genuinely drifted. Sound belongs to the simulation clock, not
       the display clock (B6). And cap voices by counting SCHEDULED END
       TIMES against the clock, never by decrementing in an "ended"
       callback -- see G9.

IF THE GAME USES AN AY-3-8912, phase 11 is a different and EASIER job, and
the beeper method above is the wrong tool because the CPU does not time
the waveform at all. Procedure:

  - Log (T-state, register, value) for every write to $FFFD (register
    select) and $BFFD (data) over driven play. Bucket by 50 Hz frame. You
    now have a standard per-frame register dump -- the same thing a .psg
    or .ay file holds.
  - Reproduce with an AY emulator in the port, or convert the dump.
    Constants you need: AY clock 1,773,400 Hz on 128K/+2/+3 (CPU/2); tone
    frequency = clock / (16 * TP) where TP is a 12-bit period from R0/R1,
    R2/R3, R4/R5; noise = clock / (16 * NP); envelope period =
    clock / (256 * EP); R7 is a mixer whose enable bits are INVERTED
    (0 = on); volume is 0-15 with bit 4 selecting the envelope.
  - VERIFY AS A REGISTER-LEVEL DIFFERENTIAL: does your player write the
    same 14 registers with the same values on the same frames as the
    original? That is strictly stronger than a frequency round-trip.
  - The tune player almost always runs from the ISR at 50 Hz, so one frame
    is one row and tempo is measured in frames -- which is also why an AY
    game is usually frame-synced, and section 8's Q10 will say so.

GATE: model versus recorded edges (or register writes) agrees across the
full parameter range including the wrap value and both extremes, as a build
gate; sound events are stamped in simulated T-states; what is not modelled
(for example ULA I/O contention) is declared with its magnitude.


PHASE 12 -- WRITE THE ENGINE
.............................

The only construction phase. Full detail in section 7. Four structural
decisions that are not stylistic:

  - MIRROR THE ORIGINAL'S DISPATCH STRUCTURE EXACTLY. One function is one
    turn of the main loop. It decrements the frame counter and calls the
    long path on wrap. The long path decrements each divider. The structure
    is not an approximation of the original's; it IS the original's. That
    is what makes a routine map possible and every later claim checkable.
  - SPEND REAL TIME IN T-STATES, not frames and not passes.
  - CONSTRAIN THE RENDERER SO PHASE 14 IS POSSIBLE AT ALL. THE RENDERER MAY
    USE ONLY PRIMITIVES A 30-LINE STUB CAN RASTERIZE. In the case study the
    engine paints exclusively through fillRect, which is why a 12-line
    fillRect shim in tools/readme_shot.js can rasterize the whole 256x192
    frame headlessly. If you reach for drawImage, putImageData, an
    offscreen canvas, CSS filters or WebGL -- all natural choices for a
    bitmap port -- none of phase 14's pixel diff, draw-call capture or
    filmstrip is buildable, and you find out after the engine is written.
    Decide this now.
  - EXPORT ONE TEST HOOK, designed in from the start. It is the only reason
    phases 13-15 are possible at all.

Build the shipped artifact from a TEMPLATE with a build step, and say
loudly in the generated file that it is generated. When inlining JSON into
a script element, escape the sequence "</" and then RE-PARSE the escaped
payload to prove the escaping held, failing the build if a placeholder is
missing.

GATE: the build prints that both payloads re-parse cleanly; the artifact
runs; the test hook is reachable.


PHASE 13 -- DIFFERENTIAL TESTING
.................................

The strongest check available to you. Full recipe in section 6.9.

GATE: N/N transitions match, WITH A PRINTED BRANCH HISTOGRAM showing every
branch exercised at least once, AND with the model that was tested being
the code you ship (6.9's cross-language step). In the case study 900/900
matched, with the census reading:

    empty slot                 540
    state 1  walking           127
    state 2  walking           116
    state 3  climbing           72
    state 4  climbing           41
    state 7  post-eat pause      3
    state 8  post-eat pause      1
                              ----
                               900
    corn eaten (buffer changed)  4   <- a SUB-COUNT, not a path

Note the shape of that: the seven path counts partition the 900; the
"corn eaten" line overlaps the walking states and must be labelled as a
sub-count or it reads as an eighth path summing to 904. Without the census
"900/900" would have been a claim about 540 early returns.


PHASE 14 -- HEADLESS TESTS AND PIXEL DIFF OF YOUR OUTPUT
.........................................................

  - Load THE BUILT ARTIFACT, not your source modules, so the build step is
    under test. Stub the minimum DOM: a lazy element map; a classList
    backed by a REAL Set so a test can assert an overlay actually went
    away; storage over a Map; a requestAnimationFrame that CAPTURES the
    callback rather than firing it.
  - THE IMPORTANT ONE: A CANVAS STUB THAT RECORDS DRAW CALLS WHEN ARMED.
    Arm it around a render call and assert on the recorded list. Several
    defect classes are invisible to state-only tests: an overlay that
    outlives its state, something that has stopped updating, something
    computed but never painted. For anything conditional, RENDER TWICE with
    only the flag changed and DIFF THE TWO DRAW LISTS.
  - Pixel-diff your rendered frame against ground truth. Sample back to
    native resolution, blank out anything whose position legitimately
    differs (your player, mid-animation entities), fit the grid, and report
    "cells differing OUTSIDE known entity positions" as a SEPARATE number.
    That split is the gate.
  - Build LOOKING tools as first-class artifacts, not throwaways: render
    consecutive frames as a FILMSTRIP; render your audio to a WAV by
    stubbing the audio API with a recorder and driving THE SHIPPED CODE
    PATH, then measure the frequencies back. In the case study every effect
    came back within 1% on pitch and duration -- and the filmstrip settled
    an argument that three rounds of numeric reasoning had got wrong.

GATE: a passing assertion count you can quote (the case study's headless
suite runs 237 checks against the built web/game.html); EVERY differing
cell in the pixel diff attributed in writing; and at least one round-trip
measurement of the SHIPPED output.


PHASE 15 -- THE PROVENANCE RECORD
..................................

Four parts, each of which earns its place:

  - THE ROUTINE MAP: original address -> what it does -> which function
    implements it. Thirty rows is plenty. This is what makes review
    possible.
  - THE FAITHFUL/ADDED BOUNDARY, stated and ENFORCED BY A TEST.
  - THE LOG OF WHAT THE ORIGINAL PROVED WRONG: a table of Assumption /
    Reality / Caught by. This is the single highest-value artifact for
    anyone doing this again, including you on the next game. The "caught
    by" column is the only honest signal about which kind of verification
    is worth investing in.
  - WHAT IS STILL OPEN, NAMED -- including which parts are YOUR OWN
    INVENTION rather than the original's behaviour. In the case study five
    items are named: one undecoded attribute path ($9332), the unexplained
    TIME-chain discrepancy, the substituted random number source, one
    behaviour the author added because the original appeared to have no
    equivalent (clearing on_lift when walking out of the shaft), and the
    death bonus refill's ranking consequence. Naming your own inventions is
    the difference between a study and a guess.


PHASE 16 -- HAND IT TO A HUMAN WHO KNOWS THE ORIGINAL
......................................................

Not optional, and not last because it is least important. In the case study
SIX OF THE THIRTEEN headline corrections were reported by a person PLAYING
the port, not found by an engineer reading code. None of them was findable
by any automated check then present. Reports of the form "he falls off the
edge slightly early" or "that feels wrong" are the highest-yield signal you
will get, because they are exactly the class no assertion is looking for.

But: A PLAY REPORT IS A BUG REPORT, NEVER A SPECIFICATION. It tells you
where to point the simulator. You then go and measure. NEVER fix a play
report by tuning a constant until it feels better.

If no such human is available, an RZX recording of the original being
played by someone competent is a partial substitute: replay it in an
emulator beside your port and compare.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. ZX SPECTRUM PLATFORM FACTS, AND WHAT TO MEASURE INSTEAD OF ASSUMING
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Each item below is stated as INVARIANT (what the hardware does, always),
then MEASURE (the specific experiment that tells you what YOUR game does
with it). The invariants you may rely on. The rest you must measure.

3.1 THE MACHINE
...............

INVARIANT: 48K Spectrum, Z80A at 3,500,000 Hz, 69,888 T-states per video
frame, 50.080 Hz. The 128K is 3,546,900 Hz, 70,908 T-states, 50.021 Hz.
Memory: ROM $0000-$3FFF, contended RAM $4000-$7FFF (shared with the ULA),
uncontended RAM $8000-$FFFF.

MEASURE: which machine and which peripherals, by the port inventory in
section 8, Q2 -- not by reading. And where the hot code lives relative to
$8000 (3.8).

3.2 THE DISPLAY FILE
....................

INVARIANT: $4000-$57FF, 6,144 bytes, and it is NOT linear.

    addr = $4000 | ((y AND $C0) shl 5) | ((y AND $07) shl 8)
                 | ((y AND $38) shl 2) | (x shr 3)

Three thirds of 64 pixel rows each ($4000, $4800, $5000); within a third,
+32 bytes per character row; +256 bytes per pixel row within a character.
Bit 7 of a byte is the LEFTMOST pixel.

A SCREEN-ADDRESS ROUTINE TAKES ONE OF THREE SHAPES, and if you look for
only the first you will conclude a game has none:

  (a) ARITHMETIC. Some combination of AND $F8, XOR 7, +$0800, shift left 5.
      This is the case study's ($5FC9 in the loader, $9538 in the game).
  (b) A LOOKUP TABLE. 192 entries (or 24 for cell-aligned work) of screen
      addresses, used by performance-sensitive games precisely to avoid the
      arithmetic. TELL: a 384-byte region of plausible $40xx-$57xx word
      values. It is trivially recognisable once you look for it.
  (c) THE INCREMENTAL DOWN-ONE-PIXEL-ROW ROUTINE, which is the single most
      widespread idiom on the machine and contains none of (a)'s shapes:
          INC H / LD A,H / AND 7 / RET NZ
          LD A,L / ADD A,32 / LD L,A / RET C
          LD A,H / SUB 8 / LD H,A / RET
      A sprite blitter that calls this per row never computes a full
      address at all after the first.

MEASURE:
  - FIND EVERY SCREEN-ADDRESS ROUTINE, not one. Different entity types
    routinely use different origins and different sub-cell resolution IN
    THE SAME FRAME. In the case study the player, the duck and the lifts
    are drawn at screen_y = 191 - y, and the ostriches at 176 - y (from
    $929C) -- fifteen pixels lower, a different routine. Get that wrong
    and every enemy sits two character rows off, on the wrong platform.
  - DETERMINE THE SIGN OF THE Y AXIS EMPIRICALLY (6.1). Score both
    orientations against a real frame. The scores are never close.
  - CHECK FOR SUB-CELL SHIFTING. A routine that ends with RR (HL) or a
    rotate loop draws at pixel resolution; one that does not draws at
    character-column resolution. In the case study the player has the
    rotate and the ostriches do not -- and the ostrich artwork compensates
    (section 4, F1).
  - CHECK THE BLITTER OPCODE. OR (HL) means sprites merge with the
    background unmasked; AND then OR means masked, and your sprite data is
    two interleaved streams and not one; XOR means the game erases by
    redrawing. In the case study it is a bare OR.

3.3 ATTRIBUTES: ONE COLOUR PER 8x8 CELL
........................................

INVARIANT: $5800-$5AFF, 768 bytes, ONE PER CELL, and LINEAR (unlike the
bitmap): addr = $5800 + row*32 + col. Bit 7 FLASH, bit 6 BRIGHT, bits 5-3
PAPER, bits 2-0 INK. A sprite CANNOT have per-pixel colour. Attribute
clash is what the game looked like; it is not a defect for you to fix.

The usual renderer shape follows: DRAW MONOCHROME BITMAPS, THEN APPLY ONE
INK PER CELL FROM THE GAME'S OWN COLOUR LOGIC, and never carry colour
inside sprite data. But there are two whole classes where that is wrong,
and you must decide which you have BEFORE writing the renderer:

  (i) SPRITE-CARRIED ATTRIBUTES. The sprite routine writes a 2x2 or 3x3
      attribute patch alongside the bitmap. Colour IS part of the sprite
      asset and must be extracted with it.
  (ii) ATTRIBUTE-ONLY OBJECTS AND GAMES. The bitmap layer is a static
      field (solid $FF, or a fixed UDG) and all motion and animation
      happen in the 768-byte attribute file. FOR THESE, PHASE 6'S PIXEL
      GATE CERTIFIES NOTHING -- "99% of play-area pixels agree" passes
      trivially with every object in the wrong place. The primary diff is
      a 768-byte ATTRIBUTE diff and the pixel diff is secondary.

MEASURE:
  - Where does colour come from? Three answers, not two: the level data;
    computed at attribute-refresh time; or written by the sprite routine.
    In the case study it is the second -- a per-tile lookup at $9555 from
    the nine-entry table at $984F, with ids >= 9 hard-coded. If your level
    data is exactly rows x cols bytes, colour is computed; if it is twice
    that, colour may be stored; if neither, read the draw routine.
  - IS BRIGHT EVER SET? Scan every attribute constant the game writes. If
    all are below $40, BRIGHT is never used and your palette components
    are 0xD7, not 0xFF. Reaching for 0xFF by default is a common and
    visible error. Confirm by sampling a real frame.
  - IS FLASH USED? Values >= $80. If so you need a 16-frame flash cycle.
  - BUILD THE INK-TO-MEANING TABLE (which ink means which object, which
    inks are unused) even for a strictly faithful port. It doubles as a
    renderer sanity check: if your play area paints an ink the game's table
    cannot produce, your renderer is wrong.

3.4 THE KEYBOARD AND JOYSTICKS
...............................

INVARIANT: eight half-rows, read from port $FE where the HIGH address byte
selects the half-row (one bit clear per half-row: $FE, $FD, $FB, $F7, $EF,
$DF, $BF, $7F). A CLEAR BIT MEANS PRESSED. Always AND $1F first: bits 5-7
are not keyboard (bit 6 is the EAR input). Two half-rows read
right-to-left ("0 9 8 7 6" and "P O I U Y").

The read can be IN A,($FE) (DB FE), or IN A,(C) / IN r,(C) (ED 78, ED 40,
ED 48, ...) with BC = $xxFE. Grep all forms, and confirm with the dynamic
port inventory (Q2). WITH B = 0, IN A,(C) SELECTS ALL EIGHT HALF-ROWS AT
ONCE and returns their AND -- the standard "is any key pressed" idiom.
Your harness must handle it (6.5).

Kempston joystick: port $1F, ACTIVE HIGH -- the opposite polarity to the
keyboard. Bits 0-4 are right, left, down, up, fire. Sinclair and
Interface 2 joysticks are keyboard rows, not a separate port.

MEASURE:
  - FIND THE GAME'S OWN CONTROL TABLE and read the masks out of it. Nearly
    every game offers redefinable keys, so the controls are a table of
    (mask, half-row select) pairs copied into working RAM at start-up.
    Find it by locating the code that reads the keys during play, seeing
    which RAM addresses it takes the pairs from, and then finding the copy
    that filled them -- in the case study six words at $732E.. copied from
    one of three tables at $8250 / $825C / $8268. Decode it and check the
    decode against any documented control scheme (phase 3 has the worked
    example, including the case where the reference was wrong).
  - THE HARNESS TRAP, and it is expensive: TO PRESS A KEY, RETURN THE
    STORED MASK VERBATIM. The key's bit is the ZERO in the mask. Inverting
    it presses every OTHER key on that half-row. In the case study an early
    timing run had the player walking LEFT when told to go right, and the
    derived walking speed was meaningless. Before measuring anything with
    the harness, ASSERT that the intended direction produces motion in that
    direction.
  - Sample input at the game's own cadence, not the display's.

3.5 SOUND
.........

INVARIANT: on a 48K the only built-in output is bit 4 of port $FE, and IT
SHARES THAT PORT WITH THE BORDER (bits 0-2) -- you cannot write one
without writing the other. Beeper pitch is set by the interval between
those writes, so the code producing them owns the CPU for that interval.

WHAT IS *NOT* AN INVARIANT: that sound therefore blocks the game. That is
a design choice, and there are four common designs -- see 11.3. Measure
which you have before you build an audio architecture around it.

The AY-3-8912 sits at ports $FFFD (register select) and $BFFD (data). A
128K always has one; a 48K MAY have one via a Fuller Box, Melodik or Timex
interface, and several 48K-era games probe for it. Many 128K games ship
BOTH a beeper and an AY path chosen at start-up. So: THE PRESENCE OF AY
WRITES IS A PROPERTY OF THE CODE, found by logging every OUT port (Q2),
not of the target machine.

MEASURE:
  - Count the confirmed speaker-write sites (11.1-11.2). One routine is a
    day's work. Dozens of inline sites, a multi-channel engine, or an AY
    path is a different order of problem.
  - Derive the model by instrumented simulation (phase 11), then verify in
    the OPPOSITE direction by rendering your own output and measuring the
    frequencies back.
  - Note what you do not model. In the case study ULA I/O contention adds a
    few T-states per port access; against beeper periods of 26H+47
    T-states (151 at H=4, rising into the thousands as H rises) that is
    well under 1% and inaudible, so it was deliberately not modelled and
    the reason was written down.

3.6 INTERRUPTS
..............

INVARIANT: the ULA raises a maskable interrupt once per frame, 50.08 Hz. In
IM 1 it dispatches to $0038 in ROM, which maintains FRAMES, KSTATE and
LAST_K and returns. In IM 2 it dispatches through the word at
(I*256 + V), where V is whatever the data bus floats to -- SO ALL 257
BYTES FROM I*256 TO I*256+256 MUST AGREE, or the vector is not
deterministic. There is no "two agreeing bytes" shortcut: two bytes cover
V=0 and V=255 only.

MEASURE:
  - DECIDE INTERRUPT-DRIVEN VERSUS FREE-RUNNING BY EXPERIMENT, NOT BY
    READING. See section 8, Q10 for the full procedure, which is the single
    most consequential measurement in the project.
  - Look for HALT in the main loop, or an EI/DI pair around the update.
  - TRACE THE INTERRUPT SERVICE ROUTINE AND LIST WHAT IT TOUCHES. Every
    variable it decrements is a timebase that something else spins on. A
    game can need BOTH a free-running update AND a 50 Hz tick. In the case
    study gameplay is free-running, but the ISR at $9C9C decrements $7373
    and the death-wait at $A960 SPINS until $7373 reaches $FF -- so a
    simulation with interrupts disabled DEADLOCKS there, which is exactly
    what happened on the first run.
  - IF IM 2: read all 257 bytes at I*256 and confirm they are identical.
    In the case study the game does LD A,$B2 / LD I,A / IM 2, and
    $B200-$B300 is a genuine 257-byte run of $9C, so the vector resolves to
    $9C9C whatever the bus floats to -- the classic run-of-identical-bytes
    trick. Dump all 257 and count them; do not sample two and assume.
    THE CASE THAT DOES NEED CARE: a game that places a valid vector only
    at I*256+$FF is relying on the bus floating to $FF, which it does on an
    unexpanded 48K. Your harness must reproduce that value rather than
    assuming a table.
  - Note WHEN the game selects its mode. Arming interrupts before that
    dispatches in the previous mode, possibly into a ROM you do not have.

3.7 THE ROM: THREE KINDS OF DEPENDENCY, ONLY ONE GREPPABLE
...........................................................

  (a) EXPLICIT: CALL, JP or RST to an address below $4000. Greppable.
      Common entries: $0556 LD-BYTES, $03B5 BEEPER (HL = pitch counter,
      DE = cycle count -- the more commonly called tone entry, because it
      needs no calculator stack), $03F8 BEEP (which does need it, via
      $2D28 STACK-A and RST $28), $0D6B CLS, RST $10 print-a-character,
      RST $28 calculator.
  (b) IMPLICIT VIA IM 1: code that reads $5C78 FRAMES, $5C08 LAST_K or the
      KSTATE bytes at $5C00.. works ONLY because the ROM's 50 Hz handler
      maintains them, and it calls nothing at all. In the case study the
      high-score name-entry screen switches to IM 1 at $A8A0, then HALTs at
      $A8B0 and reads $5C04 and $5C08 for exactly this reason.
  (c) DATA READS: the character set (the CHARS system variable holds
      $3C00, biased by 32*8, so glyphs begin at $3D00 and code reads
      LD HL,$3C00 then adds code*8 -- grep for BOTH numbers), or the ROM
      used as a table of arbitrary bytes.

MEASURE: search all three. Grep for calls below $4000; grep for reads of
$5C00-$5CB5; and look for ANY ADDRESS COMPUTATION THAT CAN LAND BELOW
$4000 -- an INC HL followed by LD H,0, or an AND $3F on a high byte. Case
(c) is the interesting one: in the case study the "random number
generator" at $9128 is not a generator at all, it advances a pointer at
$736C, forces H = 0 so the pointer walks ROM page 0, and tests bits of
whatever byte it lands on.

FOR EACH DEPENDENCY RECORD ONE OF THREE DECISIONS: reproduce it,
substitute it with the constraint stated, or declare it a gap. Never
silently approximate. And note the split from 0.5: IF GAMEPLAY CALLS ROM
PRINT, KEYBOARD OR CALCULATOR ROUTINES, USE THE REAL ROM IN YOUR HARNESS
so your measurements are real -- a dummy ROM makes them fiction -- and
ship neither it nor its behaviour. A pattern-filled $0000-$3FFF is adequate
only when you can show gameplay never calls it, as the case study could.

3.8 CONTENDED MEMORY MAKES YOUR TIMINGS A LOWER BOUND
......................................................

INVARIANT: on a 48K, $4000-$7FFF is shared with the ULA. Contention occurs
only during the 128 T-states of pixel fetch on each of the 192 display
lines. THE CONSEQUENCE PEOPLE MISS: for code in $4000-$7FFF the cost of an
IDENTICAL code path DEPENDS ON WHERE IN THE FRAME IT FALLS. It is not a
scalar penalty. Identical code runs roughly 20-50% slower on average in
contended memory, and the exact pattern varies by machine issue and clone.

Most disassembler-adjacent simulators do not model contention by default.
(SkoolKit ships both a plain Simulator and a contended CMIOSimulator; the
plain one is what most harnesses use, and what the case study used
throughout.)

MEASURE: establish where code AND WORKING DATA live relative to $8000 --
data below $8000 contends even when code does not. Then:

  - USE THE UNCONTENDED SIMULATOR FOR RATIOS, branch coverage and
    differential testing. Those are unaffected.
  - CALIBRATE ABSOLUTE RATES against a contending emulator plus human-scale
    checks.
  - IF THE HOT CODE IS ABOVE $8000, as in the case study (game code
    $8214-$CBE7; working data at $61A8 and $72xx-$73xx, which does
    contend), per-path T-state constants remain usable and the engine can
    anchor to a measured game-clock figure anyway. THE CASE STUDY NEVER RAN
    THE CONTENDED COMPARISON. Treat this paragraph as reasoning, not as a
    measured result; if it matters to you, run it both ways yourself and
    record the number.
  - IF THE HOT CODE IS BELOW $8000 -- common for 16K titles and for
    anything loading at $5CCB or $6000 -- per-path constants stop being
    available, because the same path costs different amounts at different
    points in the frame. Fallbacks: measure the MEAN over a whole frame and
    accept the loss of intra-frame phase; or abandon T-state anchoring
    entirely and calibrate to an observable in-game rate (pixels per
    second, seconds per level).

The tell for which case you are in is where the PC histogram concentrates.

3.9 BYTE-PATTERN SEARCHES OVER A MIXED CODE/DATA IMAGE
.......................................................

INVARIANT: a binary interleaves code and data, and any byte sequence you
search for will also occur inside operands, tables and graphics.

MEASURE: confirm EVERY hit by disassembling from a known instruction
boundary and checking that the boundaries land exactly on your candidate.
Two real instances from the case study: a search for the speaker-write
opcode returned three hits, of which one was a relative jump's displacement
byte followed by the next opcode; and a search for the HALT opcode ($76)
returns THIRTEEN hits in 18,900 bytes, of which exactly one ($A8B0) is an
instruction. The other twelve are the letter "v" inside "objective", "game
over player 1 level" and "you have beaten", two font glyph bitmaps, and a
word inside an address table. A 13-to-1 false positive rate is normal.

3.10 Z80 IDIOMS THAT ARE SEMANTICS, NOT STYLE
..............................................

True of any HAND-WRITTEN game of the era. See 3.11 for compiled BASIC,
where most of this is false. Full treatment in section 4, family C.

  - Extra POPs before a RET are CONTROL FLOW: "abandon the caller".
  - DEC on 0 gives 255; DJNZ with B=0 iterates 256 times. Relied upon.
  - CP n / RET nc is ">= n". CP n / RET z is "== n". AND A / RET z is
    "!= 0". Translate the exact form; a nearly-right predicate produces a
    game that mostly works, which is the hardest kind of wrong to notice.
  - 8-bit masking of a coordinate is the register width, not defensive
    coding, and other rules depend on the wrap.
  - LD (nn),HL writes TWO bytes; the second lands on whatever variable is
    next in the memory map, and adjacency is often deliberate.
  - Routines that FALL THROUGH into each other are common, and a
    disassembler's routine boundaries will hide it.
  - A whole entity's state is usually ONE CONTIGUOUS BLOCK addressed via IX
    with small offsets. Finding the LD IX,nnnn at the top of a movement
    routine hands you the entire variable map at once and turns a scavenger
    hunt into a struct definition. Look for it early.
  - LD SP,table followed by POP HL is a FAST DATA POINTER (two bytes per 10
    T-states), common in draw and decompress routines. It is not a stack
    operation, and it will destroy any sentinel you put on the stack (6.6).

3.11 CHOOSING THE RENDER MODEL, AND WHAT THIS MANUAL'S GATES ASSUME
...................................................................

Most of the pipeline -- the 672/672 buffer identity, the two-way scored
level decode, the 99%+ pixel gate, the tile lookup, the state-then-render
engine -- presupposes A SINGLE-SCREEN, TILE-MAPPED GAME WITH A STATIC
BACKGROUND. Decide early which of these you have, because it changes the
gates, not just the code.

  MODEL 1 -- STATE THEN RENDER (the case study). The game holds a tile grid
    plus entity records; the screen is a pure function of that state each
    frame. Your engine holds the same state and repaints. Every gate in
    this manual applies as written.
  MODEL 2 -- FRAMEBUFFER. The game's state IS the screen, or partly so.
    Two things force this and you must test for both:
      - AN XOR BLITTER, which erases by redrawing (Q16).
      - DISPLAY-FILE READ-BACK COLLISION: the game READS $4000-$57FF to
        decide whether something is solid. One line of harness -- a
        read-watch over the display file during driven play -- decides it,
        and it fires more often than the XOR test does. If it fires, there
        is no tile buffer to extract, phase 5 has nothing to decode, and
        your engine must maintain a real 256x192 bitplane.
  MODEL 3 -- SCROLLING PLAYFIELD. Nothing in phases 5-6 works unmodified.
    You need, in this order:
      - THE CAMERA. Recover the scroll offset by cross-correlating a real
        frame against your decoded map over dx, dy, and CROSS-CHECK the
        winner against the game's own scroll variable read out of live RAM
        (find it by the memory-diff method in 8.2). Only after that does a
        pixel diff mean anything at all.
      - THE SCROLL MECHANISM. Games scroll by moving screen memory (RL
        across 32 bytes x 192 rows, or LDIR for cell-scroll) and drawing
        only the newly exposed column. That is a stronger reason to adopt
        model 2 than the XOR blitter is, and it is not detected by reading
        the sprite routine.
      - THE PRE-SHIFT COUNT, WHICH IS A MEASUREMENT AND NOT A SURPRISE. In
        a smooth scroller, tiles and sprites exist in 2, 4 or 8 pre-shifted
        copies, and your asset extraction will report 4x or 8x as many
        sprites as the game has. COUNT THE COPIES: 8 means 1-pixel scroll
        granularity, 2 means 4-pixel, none means 8-pixel cell scroll.
        (Compare F1, where the same test found a compensation instead.)
      - THE ATTRIBUTE LAG. Attributes cannot scroll finer than 8 pixels, so
        a smooth scroller's colour visibly lags its pixels. A renderer that
        applies one ink per cell from a single grid cannot reproduce that
        unless the attribute grid gets its own independent offset.
      - OFF-SCREEN ENTITY POLICY. In a single-screen game "inactive"
        usually means "position overridden, not update skipped" (D7). In a
        scroller the opposite convention is the norm: entities outside the
        viewport are genuinely not updated, or are updated on a coarser
        divider. Measure it -- log each entity slot's update count as a
        function of its distance from the camera.
  MODEL 4 -- RASTER-TIMED EFFECTS. A game with multicolour, a border-timed
    effect, or sprites drawn while racing the beam is not reproducible by a
    state-then-render engine at frame granularity, whatever the blitter
    says. The tell is in the port inventory (Q2): repeated writes to $FE at
    regular sub-frame intervals with the border bits changing. The
    consequence is that your renderer needs a scanline model. Decide
    whether that is in scope before you promise faithfulness.

AND THE OTHER AXIS -- IS THIS EVEN HAND-WRITTEN Z80?

  - INTERPRETED BASIC. Phase 1's detokeniser IS the disassembly. Faithful
    reproduction then means reproducing ROM float behaviour, RND (seeded
    from the SEED system variable: x' = (75*(x+1) mod 65537) - 1), PAUSE
    and INKEY$ semantics -- a different job, but a much smaller one.
  - COMPILED BASIC (HiSoft Colt, Softek IS/FP, Blast, MCoder II). This
    invalidates most of 3.10 and family C, and you must detect it or you
    will waste days. Tells: stereotyped repeated CALLs into a shared
    runtime library with operands passed on the stack; no LD IX,nnnn entity
    blocks (variables live in a fixed table, often the original BASIC
    variables area); often a line-number table left in for error reporting.
    Consequences: the stack-imbalance scan (Q19) produces near-total false
    positives; "extra POPs are control flow" is simply untrue; and per-path
    T-state constants are useless, because pass cost is dominated by
    library calls and is wildly variable, so Q10 answers "neither" and you
    anchor everything to observable in-game rates.
    THE RECOVERY MOVE IS DIFFERENT AND BETTER: identify the compiler from
    its runtime signature, label the library entry points ONCE -- they are
    shared across every game from that compiler -- and the program then
    reads as a transliterated BASIC listing. The line-number table, if
    present, hands you the original structure for free.

4. THE FAILURE CATALOGUE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the heart of the manual. Every entry below is a real mistake that
was really made, on a real project, by someone competent and careful. Each
is written as:

    THE ASSUMPTION -- what was believed
    WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT -- the reason it survived review
    THE TRUTH -- what the machine code actually does
    DETECTED BY -- what caught it, which tells you what to build
    RULE -- the general form

Read the whole catalogue before you write your first gameplay rule. The
unifying observation: THIS WORK DOES NOT FAIL BY PRODUCING SOMETHING
BROKEN. It fails by producing something plausible. Every one of the entries
below looked right when it was written.

In the case study, thirteen assumptions were knocked down by the original.
SIX OF THE THIRTEEN were reported by a human playing the port, not found by
an engineer reading code. Budget for that.

(Entries are cited elsewhere as A1..A7, B1..B7, C1..C7, D1..D10, E1..E3,
F1..F3, G1..G10. The diagnostic battery in section 8 uses Q numbers, so
there is no collision: "D5" is always a failure mode, "Q5" always a
diagnostic.)

==============================================================================
FAMILY A -- DATA LAYOUT: SILENT CORRUPTION, NOT CRASHES
==============================================================================

A1  THE INVERTED AXIS
---------------------
ASSUMPTION: row 0 of the level data is the top of the screen.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: nothing in the data announces orientation, and the map
  decodes into plausible platforms EITHER WAY. In the case study both
  orientations yielded exactly 12 eggs, so even the closure invariant did
  not discriminate.
THE TRUTH: the game's y axis ran bottom-up, screen_y = 191 - game_y. The
  address routine inverts twice -- once computing the character row from
  ($38 - (y AND $38))*4, once computing the pixel row as (y AND 7) XOR 7.
  The level data is stored bottom-up BECAUSE THE WHOLE Y AXIS IS; the
  buffer is filled by a plain LDIR with no flipping anywhere. Buffer row 0
  is screen character row 23.
DETECTED BY: pixel-diffing the decoded tile layer against ONE captured
  frame, scored BOTH WAYS -- 78/218 cells (35.8%) top-down versus 210/218
  (96.3%) bottom-up.
RULE: never infer array orientation from contents. Decode both ways, score
  both against a real frame, demand a wide margin, and THEN find the
  address routine and confirm why. Also settle: origin corner, and whether
  "row" means pixel row or character row.

A2  TWO LAYOUTS, ONE DECODER
----------------------------
ASSUMPTION: the game has one bitmap format.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: the first blocks you decode are usually tiles, and they
  come out perfect.
THE TRUTH: tiles, fonts and UI were cell-major (8 consecutive bytes per 8x8
  cell); sprites were row-major (interleaved, width bytes per pixel row),
  because a different routine draws them a full 16-pixel row at a time.
DETECTED BY: a birdcage decoding as noise and the player as a smear; then
  settled by rendering BOTH interpretations of every block side by side
  into one contact sheet.
RULE: the number of layouts equals the number of draw routines. Render
  every block both ways and LOOK. Tag every block with an explicit layout
  field in your manifest so that no consumer ever guesses.

A3  THE BRIGHT PALETTE
----------------------
ASSUMPTION: use the vivid 0xFF Spectrum palette.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: it is the palette everyone remembers, and screenshots
  on the internet are often from emulators configured that way.
THE TRUTH: every attribute constant the game wrote was below 64 (the table
  at $984F holds 06 03 03 07 03 04 06 07 07), so BRIGHT was never set.
  Components are 0xD7. A real frame samples green floor at RGB(0,200,0),
  ladder (200,0,200), enemy (0,200,200), player (200,200,0), HUD paper
  (200,0,0).
DETECTED BY: an ink census over a captured frame, then closed out against
  the attribute table in the code. Note the direction of proof: the census
  establishes NOT-BRIGHT; the code establishes which entries exist; 0xD7
  is the standard non-bright rendering.
RULE: a palette is a MEASUREMENT. Read BRIGHT and FLASH out of the
  attribute-writing code, then sample a real frame and match.

A4  THE DERIVED SECOND INSTANCE
-------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: N table rows means N objects, so a missing row means a missing
  table.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: you can see two lifts on screen and find one lift
  record, so obviously there is a second table somewhere.
THE TRUTH: the second lift was DERIVED in the loader -- its y hard-coded,
  its state word computed as the first lift's minus $0800, which is exactly
  one display third and therefore exactly the 64-pixel separation you
  measured off the screen.
DETECTED BY: reading the loader instead of hunting for a table; then
  cross-checked by DECODING THE OPAQUE STATE WORD AS A DISPLAY-FILE
  ADDRESS, which resolved to the two observed screen positions.
RULE: when a table is short by a factor, look for arithmetic in the loader,
  not for another table. And an opaque 16-bit "state word" is very often a
  screen address -- decode it as one and see if it lands somewhere you
  recognise.

A5  SCREENSHOTS AS GROUND TRUTH FOR SPAWNS
------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a level-start capture gives you spawn positions.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: the geometry was exact, template matching reported full
  confidence, and every number looked clean.
THE TRUTH: a "level start" capture is already several frames in. Enemy
  counts were under-reported on two levels; every lift measured
  consistently 6 pixels risen because lifts start moving immediately; one
  entity's position was 3 pixels off because the capture came from a later
  level where it had already been released.
DETECTED BY: comparing against the game's own spawn tables, found by
  reading their loaders.
RULE: SCREENSHOTS FALSIFY, TABLES DEFINE. Anything the game tabulates beats
  anything you measured from a frame. Keep the superseded screenshot
  section labelled as such so the correction stays traceable. This is the
  canonical statement; phase 5 and phase 6 point here.

A6  THE DETECTOR THAT CANNOT SEE ITS TARGET
-------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: colour-anomaly detection will find the player in a capture.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: it works on seven levels out of eight.
THE TRUTH: on the eighth he spawns in front of a same-coloured ladder, so
  that colour stays dominant in the cell and he is invisible to the
  detector.
DETECTED BY: template-matching the EXTRACTED SPRITE BITMAP instead, which
  found him on all eight at full confidence.
RULE: detect using the artwork you extracted, not colour statistics. "Not
  present" is a hypothesis about your detector as much as about the frame.

A7  THE FALSE-POSITIVE OPCODE SEARCH
------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: grepping for an opcode byte enumerates its uses.
THE TRUTH: of three hits for the speaker write, one was a relative jump's
  displacement byte followed by the next opcode. Of thirteen hits for the
  HALT opcode, twelve were text, font glyphs and a table word.
DETECTED BY: disassembling from a known instruction boundary at each hit.
RULE: byte-pattern hits are CANDIDATES. Confirm each. Once confirmed, a
  statement like "there are exactly two speaker writes in the whole binary
  and both are in this routine" turns a whole subsystem into a
  single-routine problem, which is worth the confirmation cost. And prefer
  a dynamic port trace where one exists (11.1), because it cannot miss the
  opcode forms your grep did not think of.

==============================================================================
FAMILY B -- TIME: THE MOST EXPENSIVE FAMILY
==============================================================================

B1  THE FRAME-SYNC ASSUMPTION
-----------------------------
ASSUMPTION: the game paces itself on the 50 Hz interrupt.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: most Spectrum games do, and this one visibly sets up
  IM 2 with a vector table during start-up.
THE TRUTH: the main loop was FREE-RUNNING -- no HALT, no frame sync. The
  IM 2 handler drove a Fuller Orator speech peripheral and did not pace
  gameplay at all. But it was not inert: it decremented $7373, which the
  death-wait at $A960 SPINS on until it reaches $FF.
DETECTED BY: the first simulation run DEADLOCKING in that spin, with
  interrupts unarmed.
RULE: determine pacing empirically (section 8, Q10). And note the hybrid
  case exists: a game can need a free-running update AND a 50 Hz tick.
  Also: if a loop looks like a wait, check whether the branch target is the
  instruction itself -- in the case study a JR nz whose target was its own
  address, with nothing changing the flags, was a ONE-SHOT GUARD depending
  entirely on a value the loader wrote.

B2  THE CONSTANT-PASS-COST FALLACY
----------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: one main-loop pass costs one pass; count passes, multiply by a
  rate.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: loop counting is standard practice, the common path is
  short, and the mean rate is a real, measurable, quotable number -- the
  case study measured 399 T-states per pass, 8,783 passes/sec, averaged
  over a live level, and used it for every derived speed.
THE TRUTH: cost depends on the branch taken. Grounded 406 T-states,
  airborne 560 -- 1.38x -- because the airborne path runs the whole
  $A21C/$A2B5 chain every pass. THE GAME SLOWS DOWN WHILE THE PLAYER IS IN
  THE AIR. The mean is fine for walking speed and lethal for falls.
DETECTED BY: falls being about 1.4x too fast, and CHARACTERISTICALLY so --
  the fall delay shrinks toward a floor, so the error reads as "slow, then
  suddenly very fast". No gravity constant fixes it; the error is in the
  clock.
RULE: measure the cost of EACH PATH separately, in a live level (phase 9
  tells you how to find the paths you do not know about), and charge each
  pass its true cost in T-states. If any state changes which branches run,
  that state changes the game's speed.
  AND IT RECURS: much later the same engineer computed a "maximum possible
  score for a 24-second run" and declared two measured results impossible.
  They were not -- the score clocks tick per main-loop pass, so being
  airborne slows the clocks too (20.25 points/sec standing versus 14.80
  jumping). YOUR CATALOGUED FAILURE MODES REAPPEAR IN YOUR ANALYSIS CODE,
  not just in your engine.

B2b THE FRAME-SYNCED FORM OF THE SAME BUG
------------------------------------------
If your game is frame-synced, B2 does not go away; it changes shape, and
the manual's own advice ("run a fixed 50.08 Hz timestep") walks you into
it. THE ORIGINAL DROPS FRAMES when the update overruns 69,888 T-states --
many sprites, an explosion, a level with more enemies -- and a port running
a rigid timestep never does. Your port is then uniformly EASIER than the
original in exactly the way B2 warns about.
RULE: measure the update's T-state cost per frame as a function of
  on-screen entity count, find the frames where it exceeds one frame
  period, and reproduce the drop -- either by charging the update its
  measured cost against a frame budget, or at minimum by declaring the
  omission as a fidelity gap. AND: a fixed 50.08 Hz timestep must NOT be
  implemented as one update per requestAnimationFrame. On a 60 Hz display
  that is a 20% speed error that looks entirely right (see B6).

B3  THE MISSING DIVIDER CHAIN
-----------------------------
ASSUMPTION: an INC on a coordinate means one pixel per main-loop iteration.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: the instruction says exactly that, and nothing near it
  says otherwise.
THE TRUTH: three layers of division, stated in full at phase 10.2. Net: one
  enemy moves once per 130 x 3 x 5 = 1,950 passes. Reading the INC
  literally over-states walking by 130x and enemies by 1,950x.
DETECTED BY: measuring. Predicting from "4 pixels per 5 long passes" gives
  54 px/sec; the truth is 17.3; the full chain predicts 18.0, and the
  remaining 4% is passes spent turning around.
RULE: trace the full divider chain, write it out as a table, then measure,
  then require the two to agree. Sanity-check at human scale.

B4  NET DISPLACEMENT INSTEAD OF DISTANCE TRAVELLED
--------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: speed is (end position - start position) / elapsed.
THE TRUTH: a patroller oscillates. Over 13.6 seconds one went 104, 112, 96,
  84, 80, 64, 52, 68, 84, 100, 116 -- back near where it started.
DETECTED BY: an early run reporting exactly "0 px/sec".
RULE: accumulate absolute deltas, sample often enough not to miss a
  reversal, and when a measurement returns exactly zero, suspect the
  measurement before the phenomenon.

B5  CODE-DERIVED VERSUS MEASURED, AND WHICH SHIPS
--------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a constant read off the code is authoritative.
THE TRUTH: in the case study the timer chain read naively (housekeeping
  every 130 passes, main_sub_timer at $7345 reloading $32) predicts 6,500
  passes per displayed unit; watching the HUD digits in a live run gives
  1,181; only 1,181 produces the two-minute level the original has.
  Something in the chain does more than the naive read, and it is still
  unexplained.
DECISION TAKEN: ship the measurement, name the constant so it is obvious
  which it is, and record the discrepancy as OPEN both in the notes and in
  a comment at the constant.
RULE: when derivation and measurement disagree, THE MEASUREMENT WINS and
  the disagreement becomes a documented open question. Never quietly adjust
  the measurement toward your derivation, and never average them. A
  written-down open question is a finding; one silently resolved by picking
  the nicer number is a bug in disguise.

B6  KEYING TO DISPLAYED FRAMES INSTEAD OF THE SIMULATION CLOCK
---------------------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: sampling something "once per animation frame" is fine for
  anything that only has to look right.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: it does look right, on the machine you developed on.
THE TRUTH: anything sampled per animation frame is stamped in units of the
  display's refresh rate, so playback speed becomes (playback rate) /
  (record rate). Measured on a 20-second recording: recorded at 60 and
  replayed at 30 gave 0.500x; 60 to 144 gave 2.400x; 144 to 60 gave 0.417x
  and 67 pixels of drift at the five-second mark.
DETECTED BY: running the same code on two refresh rates.
RULE: TWO CLOCKS EXIST AND MUST NEVER BE CONFUSED. Simulation state, sound
  scheduling, timers and anything compared across machines belong to the
  SIMULATION clock. Only drawing belongs to the display clock. Sample on a
  fixed simulated cadence so the index IS the timestamp
  (index = floor(simSeconds * RATE)); because that is derived rather than
  accumulated it re-aligns for free after any state restore. Store discrete
  events stamped with the simulation's own counter -- in the case study a
  200-frame run compressed to six input changes and replayed identically at
  143 and at 286 passes per frame.

B7  THE UNRESET ACCUMULATOR
---------------------------
ASSUMPTION: resetting the game state resets the game.
THE TRUTH: the T-state accumulator was not in the reset set. One 60 Hz
  frame buys 58,333 T-states and a pass costs 406 or 560, so a FRACTION of
  a pass always carries over. Leftover credit from a previous level shifts
  where the next level's 143rd pass falls, and a replay starting from a
  different remainder diverges from the run it replays.
RULE: every accumulator with a remainder is STATE. Enumerate your reset set
  by FINGERPRINTING the simulation (flatten everything it reads or writes
  into one comparable value and assert equality), not by remembering what
  you wrote.

==============================================================================
FAMILY C -- CONTROL FLOW: THE Z80 SAYS THINGS YOUR LANGUAGE CANNOT
==============================================================================

C1  STACK UNWINDING READ AS DEAD CODE
-------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a run of POP HL with nothing done to HL is leftover or
  defensive code.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: it reads as a no-op when you are skimming for
  arithmetic, and a decompiler will not flag it.
THE TRUTH: discarding return addresses is the universal 8-bit idiom for
  "abandon the rest of this update", and EACH INSTANCE IS A GAME RULE. In
  the case study: a draw routine that does LD B,6 / POP HL six times / RET
  -- that is the player DYING; a routine with three pushes and four pops --
  eating an item abandons the rest of that entity's update, so eating costs
  two updates; an extra pop when the last collectible is taken -- LEVEL
  COMPLETE; a pop-pop-RET when the player falls below a y threshold --
  DEATH, not "stop falling"; a RET nc high on the screen -- being carried
  into the ceiling by a lift is ALSO death.
DETECTED BY: two entire death conditions being MISSING from the engine, and
  noticed in play.
RULE: in 8-bit code the stack is a control-flow device. FIND THE
  IMBALANCES MECHANICALLY (Q19 gives both the static and the dynamic scan;
  prefer the dynamic one). Any imbalance is control flow and you must be
  able to name what it abandons. Translate each into an explicit early exit
  with a comment naming the idiom. The same applies to JP (HL),
  self-modifying code, RST vectors and returning into an address table.

C2  UNCONDITIONAL FALL-THROUGH
------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: "this branch sets the animation frame to 3" means the stored
  frame is 3, so an early return there is a harmless clarification.
THE TRUTH: $9DAB sets A=3, then falls through into an unconditional
  INC A / AND 3 at $9DB2, so the stored frame is 0. A standing player draws
  frame 0, feet together. The early return froze him permanently
  mid-stride.
DETECTED BY: looking at him stand still.
RULE: follow every path to its RET. AN EARLY RETURN IN YOUR CODE IS A
  POSITIVE CLAIM THAT THE ORIGINAL HAD A BRANCH THERE. If you cannot point
  at the JR or RET, you invented one. Note also that routines frequently
  fall through into each other, and a disassembler's routine boundaries
  hide it -- in the case study the death routine falls into a
  save-the-level routine, which is what preserves collected items.

C3  THE THIRD CASE THAT DOES NOTHING
------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a dispatch arm whose body is just RET can be collapsed away.
THE TRUTH: $A21C's three-way dispatch has an arm (frameCtr != 1 AND
  airbourne == 1) that does NOTHING AT ALL, for four long passes. That is
  the teeter at the lip of a platform. It is not a no-op; it is a DURATION.
RULE: ask of every empty arm: for how many passes is this reached, and what
  does the player see during them?

C4  THE DISPATCHER THAT ONLY LOOKS LIKE IT GATES EVERYTHING
------------------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a conditional jump that skips the grounded work skips all the
  grounded work.
THE TRUTH: the jump lands ON the ladder-mount call, not before it:
      $99C1  LD A,(farmer_airbourne)
      $99C4  AND A
      $99C5  JR nz,$99D9
      ...    grounded work, including CALL z,$B34C at $99D6
      $99D9  CALL $9E98        <-- the ladder MOUNT, the jump's target
  So the mount runs UNCONDITIONALLY -- which is why the player can grab a
  ladder mid-jump, and why mounting then clears the airborne flag ($9F2E)
  and cancels the jump.
RULE: read the dispatcher to the END and note exactly where each branch
  lands. A conditional jump that skips a block does not skip what follows
  the block. Draw the control-flow graph rather than reading top to bottom.

C5  BYTE WRAPPING IS LOAD-BEARING
---------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: DEC (HL) / RET nz counts down to zero and proceeds.
THE TRUTH: it is a BYTE. Decrementing 0 gives 255 -- a full 256-pass wait.
  In the case study this happens twice and both are visible: at take-off
  (the counter starts at 0) and at the apex (the phase is set to 0), where
  the original visibly hangs. Modelling the counter as a signed integer
  that goes negative deletes BOTH pauses.
RULE: model every register at its native width and mask on every write.
  Comment where a wrap is relied upon. An unbounded-integer language
  silently deletes behaviour an 8-bit machine could not avoid.

C6  THE SINGLE-TARGET WRITE THAT ISN'T
--------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: eating an item stalls one named timer.
THE TRUTH: LD HL,$FFFF / LD (timer),HL is a SIXTEEN-BIT store landing on
  TWO ADJACENT sub-timers -- in the case study the level clock and the
  bonus clock, both stalled for about 0.7 seconds. Miss it and the item
  feels pointless.
RULE: check the OPERAND WIDTH of every store. The second byte lands on
  whatever is next in the memory map, and adjacency in a hand-built 8-bit
  variable map is usually deliberate. Build the variable map first so you
  can see what a 16-bit write splashes onto.

C7  THE MISLABELLED ROUTINE
---------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a careful third-party disassembly's routine titles are facts.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: in the case study the reference was excellent --
  9,748 data bytes cross-checked with ZERO mismatches, 152 labels, 96
  routines titled, every graphic dimensioned.
THE TRUTH: only about 10% of instructions carried comments, so the titles
  were readings, not derivations. Two were wrong, and both mattered: $9E66,
  titled "checks if there is enough headroom for a jump", is the
  player/entity OVERLAP TEST (a 13-wide, 28-tall box); $734A/$734B, marked
  "Unknown variable", are the duck's X and Y velocities, signed and clamped
  to plus or minus 5. A third, the middle control table's comment, named
  the wrong alternate jump key (phase 3).
RULE: treat third-party labels as HYPOTHESES WITH A USEFUL PRIOR. Confirm
  by call sites and by watching the variables change in a live run. Prepare
  corrections and send them upstream.

==============================================================================
FAMILY D -- RULE SHAPE: THE PREDICATE IS SUBTLY DIFFERENT
==============================================================================

These make a port FEEL wrong while every screenshot looks right. No visual
test finds them. Only the code finds them, or a player who knows the
original.

D1  THE REASONABLE REIMPLEMENTATION (THE ARCHETYPE)
----------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: "walk past the end of a platform and you fall" -- so check for
  ground under the feet each step, and if there is none, start falling.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: it is what any designer would specify, it is what the
  game appears to do, and it produces a playable game.
THE TRUTH: the real routine is $B34C, twenty-one instructions, and three of
  its properties a reimplementation will never produce -- a gate that skips
  the check entirely on cell-aligned positions (giving one pixel of
  overhang per edge), a support test that is ">= 5" so that a decorative
  object supports you while collectibles do not, and an entry into a
  TRANSIENT state with a four-tick hesitation rather than a straight drop.
  Section 9 carries it end to end.
DETECTED BY: a player saying "he falls off the edge slightly early". That
  one sentence is the only reason anyone looked at the routine at all.
RULE: NEVER WRITE A RULE FROM PLAUSIBILITY. Find the routine, read all of
  it, translate line by line with the original in a comment beside your
  code. If you cannot find it yet, mark the rule PROVISIONAL in both the
  code and the notes. And listen to imprecise reports: "slightly early",
  "feels wrong" are the highest-yield signal you will ever get.

D2  ONE BOX FITS ALL
--------------------
ASSUMPTION: the game has a collision routine and entities use it.
THE TRUTH: at least two, of different shapes. In the case study the ground
  enemies use $9E66, a 13-wide, 28-tall box that is ASYMMETRIC and offset
  upward; the flying entity uses a plain symmetric window of plus or minus
  8 and 9. And one draw routine performs its own collision test INLINE
  while drawing.
DETECTED BY: play feel. No static check would flag it.
RULE: count the collision tests before writing one. Check whether draw
  routines test too. Write the numbers in a comment at each, naming the
  entity.

D3  MIRROR-IMAGE CODE THAT IS NOT A MIRROR IMAGE
--------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: the left and right paths are the same code with a sign flipped.
THE TRUTH: consistently asymmetric. In the case study: walking right uses
  column (x-1) >> 3 rather than x >> 3; moving left tests column c while
  right tests c+2; ladder dismount probes col-1 on the left and col+2 on
  the right; the edge bounce triggers at x mod 8 >= 4 moving left but >= 3
  moving right; and even the SOUNDS differ by one cycle of length in each
  direction (L=5 left, L=6 right), for no visible reason.
RULE: diff the two directions instruction by instruction and implement both
  literally. Where they differ for no reason you can find, KEEP THE
  DIFFERENCE and comment that you kept it deliberately.

D4  TWO ROUTINES, ONE CELL, DIFFERENT RULES
--------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: "can I stand here" and "can I land here" are the same question.
THE TRUTH: $B34C and $A356 test the SAME CELL with DIFFERENT predicates,
  and $B34C additionally skips its test on aligned positions. The
  consequence is a position where the player stands on nothing and from
  which a vertical jump CANNOT LAND, so he drops to the floor below. The
  landing routine has its own shape entirely: tile 0 means keep falling;
  tile 5 (floor) means land; tile >= 3 (egg, corn, cage) means KEEP FALLING
  (so the object that supports standing does not catch a fall); a ladder
  half (1 or 2) lands only if the adjacent half's cell is floor; and
  landing additionally requires (y+1) mod 8 == 0, an 8-pixel snap.
DETECTED BY: a static tool that ENUMERATED EVERY DISAGREEMENT between the
  two predicates on every level, followed by running the real Z80 at each
  x across an edge and diffing the engine's answers position by position.
RULE: wherever two routines consult the same data with different
  predicates, ENUMERATE THE DISAGREEMENTS EXHAUSTIVELY, decide in writing
  whether each is a bug or a feature, then freeze the decision as a test.
  This is the highest-value automated check available, because it finds
  quirks you did not know to look for.

D5  THE OVER-STRICT PREDICATE
-----------------------------
ASSUMPTION: you can only step off a ladder onto floor.
THE TRUTH: the real test accepted anything in a range of ids -- ladders and
  collectibles counted as support. Requiring floor makes the dismount
  SILENTLY FAIL at plenty of legitimate places, and the valid exits are
  already sparse.
RULE: translate comparisons literally and preserve their exact form. Write
  the accepted range out in a comment. A nearly-right predicate produces a
  game that mostly works, which is the hardest kind of wrong to notice.

D6  RESPAWN RESTORES EVERYTHING
-------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: losing a life reloads the level.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: the death routine plainly plays a tune, waits and
  clears the screen -- everything a reload does.
THE TRUTH: it FALLS THROUGH into a routine that copies the CURRENT level
  buffer into the player's saved slot, and the respawn copies it back. So
  items already collected STAY COLLECTED across a death; only entities and
  timers reset. Restoring the level makes the game markedly easier than the
  original.
DETECTED BY: killing the player in the simulator and counting items before
  and after -- 8 and 8.
RULE: verify what survives a life loss by DIFFING MEMORY ACROSS THE
  TRANSITION -- the whole level buffer, the score block, the timers, the
  entity slots -- not by reasoning about what "restart" ought to mean. Do
  the same for level completion, last life, and any difficulty transition.
  SECOND-ORDER CONSEQUENCE worth noting: because respawn also refills the
  bonus and the clock, a death hands the player free points. That is
  faithful and it stays -- but anything that later RANKS runs must charge
  the refund back, or a scrappy run outranks a clean one.

D7  THE CAGED ENTITY THAT IS STILL RUNNING
-------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a visibly caged or inactive entity can have its update skipped.
THE TRUTH: the update ran IN FULL every tick -- velocities kept
  accumulating toward their clamps, the wing-flap toggle kept flipping --
  and only the POSITION was forced back by a separate line. Return early
  and it sits frozen; it should be flapping in there.
RULE: "inactive" usually means "position overridden", not "update skipped".
  Find the exact statement that suppresses the entity and suppress exactly
  that. IN A SCROLLING GAME THE OPPOSITE CONVENTION IS THE NORM -- see
  3.11 -- so measure rather than assume either way.

D8  THE ENTITY WITH ITS OWN DRAW ORIGIN
----------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: one screen-address convention per game.
THE TRUTH: in the case study most things draw at screen_y = 191 - y and the
  ground enemies at 176 - y, fifteen pixels lower, from a different routine
  ($929C). Getting it wrong puts every enemy two character rows off, on the
  wrong platform.
RULE: derive the screen-address formula PER DRAW ROUTINE. This is the
  canonical statement; 3.2 and 7.3 point here.

D9  THE SPRITE THAT DOES NOT ANIMATE (AND THE ONE THAT ANIMATES ON
    POSITION)
-------------------------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: animation frames advance on a counter.
THE TRUTH: in the case study the airborne player is drawn with a FIXED
  frame ($A30F draws frame 1), so the legs do not cycle during a jump; and
  the enemy's frame choice is POSITIONAL, not temporal -- two climb states
  share a base, climbing frames alternate on (y AND 4), walking frames
  alternate on (x AND 4), and an eating state indexes directly. The draw
  routine also walks its data BACKWARDS.
RULE: frame selection is a rule like any other, frequently different per
  state and per entity. Read the draw routine; do not assume a counter.

D10 THE OFFSET INSIDE THE DRAW
------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a sprite is drawn at the entity's position.
THE TRUTH: one state subtracts 8 from the address just before drawing, so
  the left-facing eating frame is drawn a whole character further left --
  which is how the bird's neck reaches into the cell it is emptying. The
  right-facing version is drawn in place. Without it the bird pecks at
  empty air.
RULE: read to the END of the draw routine. Per-state draw offsets are cheap
  on a Z80, invisible in the state, and obvious on screen.

==============================================================================
FAMILY E -- SUBSTITUTION: THINGS YOU CANNOT COPY
==============================================================================

E1  THE STATISTICALLY NAIVE RANDOM SUBSTITUTE
----------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: the original's randomness needs a ROM you do not have, so any
  decent pseudo-random source will do.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: an LCG passed a casual eyeball test and the enemies
  still walked around plausibly. The failure was INVISIBLE IN AGGREGATE AND
  TOTAL IN DETAIL.
THE TRUTH, two facts: (a) $9128 was not a generator at all -- it advanced a
  pointer at $736C, forced the high byte to 0 so the pointer walked ROM
  page 0, and tested bits of whatever byte it landed on; (b) BOTH DECISION
  BITS IN ONE UPDATE CAME FROM THE SAME BYTE -- bit 0 gated the ladder
  check, bit 1 chose which direction to try first. Drawing two independent
  bits is simply wrong; they are correlated.
  THE FATAL INTERACTION: every LCG modulo a power of two has a PERIOD-2
  LOW BIT; the game gates on BIT 0; and the enemy's own (x AND 4) also
  flips on every 4-pixel move. The two lock ANTI-PHASE and the enemy then
  never tests for a ladder at all. One table produced a perfect
  1010101010... and left a particular enemy unable to climb for its entire
  life.
DETECTED BY: noticing an enemy that never climbed; then made systematic by
  a tool that prints the bit-0 stream over 40 draws, counts alternations
  and flags "ALTERNATING - unusable", and by a behavioural test that runs
  every enemy on every level for 200,000 steps asserting each reaches a
  climbing state. A related check counted states where an enemy stands over
  empty space: 1,019 before the fixes, ZERO after.
FIX: a xorshift sampled from its MIDDLE bits, (s >> 11) AND 0xFF, so bits 0
  and 1 carry no short-period structure -- and preserving the
  two-bits-from-one-byte correlation.
RULE: when you substitute a generator, first CHARACTERISE HOW THE
  ORIGINAL'S OUTPUT IS CONSUMED -- which bit, at what cadence, against what
  other periodic quantity in the game -- and test the substitute against
  that consumption pattern, not against a generic randomness test. NEVER
  use an LCG modulo 256 as a bit source. State clearly in your notes that
  this is a place the port deliberately does not reproduce the original,
  and why.

E2  THE SIMULATION ENVIRONMENT THAT DIFFERS FROM THE REAL ONE
--------------------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: results from the simulator are results from the machine.
THE TRUTH: you get the machine you built. In the case study two facts were
  written at the top of the harness: no Spectrum ROM is shipped, so
  $0000-$3FFF holds a deterministic pattern -- gameplay never calls the ROM
  (only the tune routine does) but the enemy decision source READS page 0,
  so enemy decisions in simulation are deterministic rather than authentic,
  while TIMING IS UNAFFECTED, which is the property every timing
  measurement rests on. And interrupts must be armed, but only after the
  game selects IM 2, or they dispatch in IM 1 into the dummy ROM -- yet
  they must be armed, or an ISR-driven spin deadlocks.
RULE: write down at the top of your harness exactly what your substitute
  environment does and does not preserve, and check every measurement
  against that list before believing it.

E3  HAND-COUNTING T-STATES
--------------------------
ASSUMPTION: a ten-instruction routine's timing can be worked out on paper.
THE TRUTH: the two halves of the case study's square wave are NOT equal --
  the loop overhead all falls on the low half (high = 13H+14, low = 13H+33)
  -- and H=0 means 256, not 0, because DJNZ with B=0 wraps. Those are
  exactly the two places a hand count goes wrong.
DETECTED BY: running the real routine with a port tracer and fitting the
  model to recorded edges. Then closing the loop the other way: rendering
  the SHIPPED engine's audio to a WAV through a stubbed audio API and
  measuring the frequencies back -- every effect within 1%.
RULE: never hand-count cycles for anything that matters. Instrument the
  emulator at the hardware boundary with T-state stamps and derive the
  model from the trace; then verify in the opposite direction through the
  code you actually ship.

==============================================================================
FAMILY F -- "IMPROVEMENT": FIXING WHAT WAS ALREADY COMPENSATED
==============================================================================

F1  PRE-SHIFTED ART DEFEATS SMOOTHING (THREE WRONG ATTEMPTS IN A ROW)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the canonical statement of 1.2. Read it before you "improve"
anything.
ASSUMPTION: the enemy draw address is cell-aligned (the sub-cell x really
  IS discarded -- the address comes from x >> 3 with no rotate, while the
  player's routine does have a rotate), and enemies move 4 pixels at a
  time, so half their positions are 8k+4; therefore they lurch and should
  be interpolated.
WHY IT LOOKS RIGHT: every step of that reasoning is CORRECT. Only the
  conclusion is wrong.
THE TRUTH: THE WALK SPRITES ARE PRE-SHIFTED 4 PIXELS INSIDE THEIR 16-PIXEL
  ART CELL, EXACTLY CANCELLING IT. Verified byte-exactly:
  (sprite_stand >> 4) == sprite_walk on rows 3 to 11 (neck and torso); only
  rows 0-2 and 12-15 are genuine animation. The invariant is
  (x >> 3)*8 + the art's built-in ink offset (0 or 4) == x, ALWAYS. The
  hardware throws the sub-cell x away and the ARTWORK PUTS IT STRAIGHT
  BACK. So a walking enemy already glides evenly, and the frame alternation
  on (x AND 4) is the SAME MECHANISM -- the second frame conveys the
  half-step the sprite cannot make.
THE THREE ATTEMPTS, all reverted:
  (1) ease toward the RAW x -- parks a climbing enemy 4 pixels beside the
      ladder, because the mount forces x = 8k+4 while the draw uses 8k;
  (2) ease toward the SNAPPED x and key the frame off the eased position --
      the snapped target only changes on the stand update, so the sprite
      glides while showing the stand pose, and keying the frame off the
      eased value STARVES the stride, because the eased value settles on
      multiples of 8 where bit 2 is always clear. The walk pose showed on 1
      frame in 14 against the original's 7 in 14;
  (3) the eased draw manufactures a visible MOONWALK -- body-delta
      histograms walking left were original {-4: 8, 0: 111} versus eased
      {-4: 4, -1: 33, 0: 78, +4: 4}, those +4 entries being the body
      jerking 4 pixels BACKWARDS once per stride, four and a half times a
      second.
DETECTED BY: a FILMSTRIP of consecutive frames. The mistake, three times,
  was reasoning about numbers instead of looking at the animation.
RULE: WHEN A ROUTINE LOOKS LIKE IT IS CUTTING A CORNER, FIND OUT WHAT
  COMPENSATES BEFORE FIXING IT. Check the ART for the fix before you add
  one. (In a scroller the same test means something else entirely -- 3.11.)

F2  THE QUIRK THAT IS THE FEEL OF THE GAME
-------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTION: an asymmetry, a one-pixel inconsistency, or a state where the
  character stands on nothing is a bug to clean up.
THE TRUTH: the skipped edge check, the aligned-overhang perch, the
  four-pass teeter, the 8-pixel landing snap and the velocity flip that
  kicks the player off a platform edge ARE the game. Remove them and you
  have something that passes every screenshot test and does not feel like
  the original.
DEFENCE: lock each verified quirk into a regression test WITH THE
  ORIGINAL'S OWN ANSWER IN THE ASSERTION and the tool invocation that
  produced it in a comment.
RULE: faithfulness includes the flaws. A quirk without a test will be
  removed by someone acting in good faith.

F3  THE INK-TO-MEANING TABLE
----------------------------
Even for a strictly faithful port, write down which ink means which object
and which inks are unused. It is how you sanity-check your own renderer: if
your play area paints an ink the game's colour table cannot produce, your
renderer is wrong. (In the case study: green floor, magenta ladders AND
corn, white eggs, yellow player and scenery, cyan enemies and lifts, red as
HUD paper only, blue unused.)

==============================================================================
FAMILY G -- METHOD FAILURES: PROCESS, INSTRUMENT, TEST
==============================================================================

These cost more elapsed time than the interpretation failures, because a
wrong instrument sends the investigation somewhere else entirely.

G1  THE UNINSTRUMENTED INSTRUMENT
---------------------------------
A recording appeared to over-sample by 20% and several steps of a live
investigation went into chasing it. It was not over-sampling: a diagnostic
divided the recording length by 5 when each sample had grown to 6 numbers
wide. The recording had been exactly right the whole time. Fixed by
dividing by the engine's own exported group-size constant.
RULE: INSTRUMENT THE INSTRUMENT. When a measurement is surprising, verify
  the MEASURING code before the measured code -- surprising measurements
  are more often wrong than surprising behaviour is. Never hard-code a
  stride, width or record size in a diagnostic; read it from the thing
  being measured.

G2  THE INVERTED INPUT MASK
---------------------------
See 3.4. A harness that silently presses the wrong key produces beautifully
precise nonsense.
RULE: take the input encoding from the GAME'S OWN TABLE, and assert that
  the intended direction produces motion in that direction before measuring
  anything with it.

G3  THE TRACE THAT PROVES NOTHING
---------------------------------
"900/900 transitions match" means nothing if the climbing and eating
branches never ran.
RULE: PRINT THE BRANCH HISTOGRAM BESIDE EVERY MATCH RATE. This is the
  canonical statement; phase 13 carries the case study's actual census and
  the warning about sub-counts that do not partition. If a branch has a
  handful of hits, construct a scenario that forces it. A differential test
  is a claim about the paths it exercised and nothing else.

G4  SNAPSHOT GRANULARITY IN A DIFFERENTIAL TEST
------------------------------------------------
Entities EAT items, mutating the shared level buffer, so reading the buffer
once at the end compares your model against the wrong world.
RULE: capture complete input state and complete output state PER
  TRANSITION -- anything the routine can mutate is part of both -- and
  replay each transition independently from its own captured input, so a
  divergence at step N cannot silently corrupt every later comparison.
  IF THE ROUTINE IS SELF-MODIFYING, ITS OWN BYTES ARE PART OF THE MUTABLE
  WORLD, and the operand that decides its behaviour may have been patched
  by a different routine many passes earlier -- so a per-transition replay
  re-seeds the wrong code and the test passes or fails for reasons
  unrelated to your model. Run Q3 before trusting any differential.

G5  TESTS CONTAMINATED BY STATE LEFT BY EARLIER TESTS
------------------------------------------------------
Four real instances from one file: (a) a fall-rate measurement dropped the
player straight into an enemy, so he died before any samples appeared;
(b) a deflection test measured the wrong walker because a second, decorative
actor had brushed the same scenery more recently; (c) a block that ended
mid-death with a timer counting broke the block after it; (d) the fractional
T-state accumulator carried between runs.
RULE: every measurement must DECLARE AND ASSERT its preconditions, and
  every block must leave the world in a state the next can start from. In a
  simulation, "leftover state" includes entities you forgot exist, timers
  mid-count, persistent storage and fractional accumulators.

G6  TESTS THAT CANNOT SEE THE SCREEN
------------------------------------
Several defects were on the PRESENTATION side and invisible to state-only
tests: an overlay that survived a state restore, a figure that had stopped
updating, an element computed but never painted.
RULE: stub the canvas so it RECORDS DRAW CALLS when armed, and assert on
  the recorded list. For anything conditional, render TWICE with only the
  flag changed and DIFF the two draw lists. And MEASURE THE DRAWN
  QUANTITY, not the underlying one -- a test counting sign reversals in a
  vertical velocity happily reported a hop as flight, because the visible
  motion lived in a drawn offset rather than in the physics. (This only
  works if the renderer obeys the phase-12 primitive constraint.)

G7  REASONING ABOUT NUMBERS INSTEAD OF LOOKING
-----------------------------------------------
Three consecutive wrong turns on one animation were all number-reasoning; a
filmstrip settled it in one look. The same pattern recurs on every piece of
art: a decorative frond read as a ladder, a bird's head read as a mushroom
at 8 pixels until the beak stuck out.
RULE: for anything visual, render a filmstrip of consecutive frames and
  LOOK at it -- thirty lines of code that settles arguments histograms
  cannot. Complement: for anything visible only in aggregate, PRINT THE
  HISTOGRAM. It is what turns "it looks a bit odd" into "there is a +4".

G8  FLAKY TESTS FROM SAME-MAGNITUDE SIGNALS
--------------------------------------------
A global periodic effect and a per-object idle oscillation were the same
size, so a test sampling at different moments could not distinguish them.
RULE: when two signals of comparable amplitude share an output, hold time
  constant -- take all readings at a single instant -- and vary only the
  input under test.

G9  THE RESOURCE FREED BY A CALLBACK THAT NEVER FIRES
------------------------------------------------------
A voice cap incremented on start and decremented in an "ended" callback; in
a headless harness that callback never fires, so the counter only climbed
and the game went silent after 28 sounds. (Sibling defect, same family:
input transitions stamped with the pass counter BEFORE the pass they
affect, so playback using >= instead of > diverged at the first change of
direction and nowhere earlier.)
RULE: a callback you cannot guarantee should never be the only thing that
  frees a resource -- count scheduled END TIMES against the clock instead,
  because a resource freed by arithmetic cannot leak. And write down, at
  both the recording and the playback site, whether a timestamp is taken
  before or after the step it describes.

G10 STATE THAT IS NOT IN THE SNAPSHOT
--------------------------------------
A restore feature restored the simulation but not the pass counter that
recorded input is stamped against, so the recording afterwards pointed at
passes that never happened.
RULE: enumerate simulation state BY MEASUREMENT, not memory: fingerprint
  everything the simulation reads or writes into one comparable value and
  assert that a restore reproduces it exactly. Then assert the property
  that actually matters -- run 30, mark, run 90, rewind 90, state == mark
  exactly, run 90 again, state == later exactly. A DETERMINISM PROPERTY IS
  ALWAYS STRONGER THAN AN EQUALITY PROPERTY.

5. THE EVIDENCE LADDER, AND WHAT "DONE" MEANS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5.1 THE LADDER
..............

Ten rungs. Each is defined by the KIND OF ORACLE it uses, not by the
subject matter, so the ladder transfers to any port in any language. NEVER
QUOTE A CLAIM AT HIGHER CONFIDENCE THAN ITS RUNG.

R0  IT LOOKS RIGHT.
    PROVES: nothing. CANNOT PROVE: anything at all.
    USE: as a trigger to go and measure. Never as evidence.

R1  A THIRD PARTY SAYS SO (a disassembly's prose, a magazine, a wiki, your
    own memory of playing it in 1984).
    PROVES: a plausible hypothesis, worth a prior.
    CANNOT PROVE: correctness. Three reference statements were wrong in the
    case study.
    USE: to decide where to look first.

R2  A STRUCTURAL INVARIANT HOLDS IN THE EXTRACTED DATA (zero unrecognised
    values; exactly the item count the game is known to have).
    PROVES: your format hypothesis is self-consistent and closed.
    CANNOT PROVE: orientation, layout, or meaning. In the case study the
    12-egg invariant held under BOTH row orders.
    USE: as the gate on phase 5.

R3  STATIC BYTE CROSS-CHECK against an independent copy of the same binary.
    PROVES: same tape, right ORG, the reference usable as a map.
    CANNOT PROVE: any instruction's meaning. Not one instruction is
    checked.
    NOT AVAILABLE AT ALL if you took the snapshot route (Q1) for any
    decompressed or decrypted region -- there is no tape-byte counterpart
    to compare against. When R3 is gone, the pixel diff and the closure
    test have to carry the whole load, and you must say so.

R4  LITERAL SIMULATION OF YOUR OWN MODEL (your engine runs, and behaves
    self-consistently).
    PROVES: your model is internally consistent.
    CANNOT PROVE: that it matches the original. Internal consistency will
    never catch an error of 130x.

R5  WHOLE-OUTPUT CAPTURE COMPARISON (pixel-diff your rendered frame against
    a real frame).
    PROVES: the static picture matches, to the precision of your diff.
    CANNOT PROVE: any behaviour over time; and A LIT/UNLIT DIFF IS BLIND TO
    COLOUR, so ink needs a separate census -- which for an attribute-driven
    game (3.3) means the attribute diff is primary and this rung is
    secondary.

R6  DIRECT MEASUREMENT OF THE ORIGINAL EXECUTING (T-states per pass,
    pixels per second, register contents at a chosen PC).
    PROVES: the quantity, in the environment you built.
    CANNOT PROVE: that your model reproduces it, or that anything depending
    on a substituted environment is authentic.

R7  POINT DIFFERENTIAL: ask the original and your model the SAME question
    at a chosen point and compare (the edge-jump table at each x).
    PROVES: agreement at those points.
    CANNOT PROVE: agreement anywhere else. Enumerate the whole
    neighbourhood, not one interesting case.

R8  TRACE DIFFERENTIAL: step the original, snapshot the world before and
    after every invocation of a routine, replay each through your model,
    compare every field.
    PROVES: your model is exact ON THE PATHS THE TRACE EXERCISED.
    CANNOT PROVE: anything about paths that did not run (hence the branch
    histogram is not optional), and nothing at all about your SHIPPED code
    unless the model under test is the shipped code (6.9 step 8).

R9  ROUND-TRIP RE-MEASUREMENT OF THE SHIPPED ARTIFACT: render your engine's
    audio to a WAV and measure the frequencies back; render its frames and
    diff them; run the headless suite against the BUILT file.
    PROVES: the thing you ship does what your model says.
    CANNOT PROVE: that the model was right -- that is R6 to R8's job.

THE CRITICAL SEAM: R6 to R8 verify a MODEL. R9 and the headless harness
verify THE THING YOU SHIP. Ports rot in the gap between them -- a correct
model, a build step that drops an asset, an engine path that never calls
the verified function. Test both ends.

5.2 THE DONE RULE
.................

    A rule is DONE when the original has been made to answer for it: when
    every number in it traces to a named address, was produced by MEASURING
    or BYTE-COMPARING the original rather than by reasoning about it, has
    been checked at the highest rung its kind admits, is pinned by a test
    whose expected value came from somewhere other than the implementation,
    and whose remaining unknowns are written down and attributed.

Five checks:

  1 PROVENANCE. The rule cites an address, at a stated ORG. If you cannot
    name the instruction, the rule is provisional and must say so.

  2 DERIVATION. Measured, not hand-counted. If you also derived it and the
    two disagree, the measurement ships and the gap is logged (B5).

  3 RUNG. Per kind of artifact, the highest rung that applies:
      extracted data ....... byte compare + closure invariant + pixel diff
      spawn tables ......... live-RAM cross-check against a running game
      a routine with I/O ... trace differential WITH a path histogram
      a quirk .............. point differential over the whole
                             neighbourhood, not the one case you noticed
      a timing figure ...... T-state measurement plus an independent
                             cross-check, at least one of them external or
                             human-scale
      anything reaching the screen or speaker ... round-trip capture
      a colour ............. the attribute-writing code path, confirmed
                             against an ink census on a real frame

  4 A TEST THAT WOULD FAIL IF SOMEBODY "FIXED" IT. The expected value must
    come from one of exactly four legitimate sources:
      (a) hard-coded measured ground truth, WITH the tool invocation that
          produced it in a comment;
      (b) an independent re-derivation inside the test (in the case study
          this caught an error in the PROSE while the code was right);
      (c) a cross-quantity invariant (footstep count versus pixels
          travelled);
      (d) a universal property (replay determinism; artwork byte identity;
          subset membership; queue headroom).
    NEVER from calling the code under test.

  5 DECLARED LIMITS. What is not modelled, and what your substitutions
    cost.

5.3 THE NEGATIVE FORM, WHICH MATTERS MORE
..........................................

A rule is NOT done because:
  - it looks right;
  - it plays right;
  - a test passes, if you wrote the test from the same misunderstanding;
  - a third-party disassembly says so;
  - it matches at one point;
  - the differential reports 100%, if you did not print the path histogram
    -- in the case study 540 of those 900 transitions were empty slots
    returning early;
  - the differential reports 100% against a model that is not the code you
    ship.

ANYTHING NOT DONE IS AN ASSUMPTION. Assumptions live in a table with their
status and with what would settle them. When one is knocked down it moves
to the assumption-versus-reality table with its "caught by" column filled
in.

6. BUILDING THE SIMULATOR HARNESS AND THE DIFFERENTIAL TEST
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The harness is about 150 lines on top of an existing Z80 simulator library
(SkoolKit's Simulator is a good choice in Python; any library that exposes
memory, registers, a T-state counter and per-instruction stepping will do).
It is the highest-leverage code in the project. Build it before you write
any gameplay.

6.1 THE MEMORY IMAGE
....................

FOR AN UNPROTECTED, UNCOMPRESSED GAME: do NOT run the loader. BUILD THE
POST-LOADER IMAGE DIRECTLY:

  - allocate 64K;
  - copy each block to the load address you recovered in phase 2;
  - replay the loader's SIDE EFFECTS by hand, each with a comment naming
    the instruction it came from. In the case study that is three writes:
    $0001 into $5C3D (the one-shot guard), and $5DC0 into each of $5C63 and
    $5C65 (STKBOT and STKEND, which the tune routine writes through);
  - set SP, the interrupt mode and I to whatever the loader left.

You are ASSERTING THE LOADER'S POSTCONDITION. Phase 2 is what tells you
what it is; phase 2's dynamic confirmation run is the ONE time you execute
the loader.

FOR A PROTECTED, ENCRYPTED, COMPRESSED OR CUSTOM-LOADER GAME: do NOT try to
run the loader in this harness. It cannot work, and the reason is worth
understanding because it is the same reason twice: 6.2 fills $0000-$3FFF
with a pattern, so a ROM loader's CALL $0556 runs into garbage; and 6.5
returns $FF for unknown ports, so a custom loader's edge-detection loop
reads EAR permanently high and never decodes a bit. Running a real loader
needs the actual ROM plus a tape-player model driving bit 6 of port $FE
with T-state-accurate pulse widths reconstructed from the TZX block
parameters. That is a project of its own and you do not need it.

    USE A FULL EMULATOR OR A TAPE-TO-SNAPSHOT TOOL INSTEAD.
    SkoolKit's tap2sna.py, or Fuse / ZEsarUX with a breakpoint at the
    transfer into the game and a .z80 or .sna export. Load THAT as your
    memory image. The artifact you want is the same either way -- a 64K
    image plus the machine state -- and you never have to understand the
    packer.

Two consequences of the snapshot route, both of which you must write down:

  - R3 IS GONE for every decompressed or decrypted region, so phase 3's
    byte cross-check cannot be met there and the pixel diff plus the
    closure test carry the whole load.
  - RUNTIME DECRYPTION IS NOT THE SAME AS LOAD-TIME PACKING. Multi-stage
    schemes decrypt a routine, run it, and re-encrypt it, so no single
    snapshot contains all the plaintext. Recovery is to break on
    transfer-of-control INTO each region and dump it there. And if code
    executes from the display file or the printer buffer at $5B00, the
    static image contains no disassemblable copy at all, so phases 3-5 must
    run after the snapshot rather than before it. Q3 tells you which of
    these you have.

6.2 ROM SUBSTITUTION
....................

You will not ship a Spectrum ROM. Whether your HARNESS needs a real one is
a separate question, answered by Q4.

If gameplay never calls the ROM, fill $0000-$3FFF with a DETERMINISTIC
pattern (in the case study, the first 256 bytes were set to (i*37+11) AND
255 and the rest zeroed) and write the consequences in the module
docstring:

  - what still depends on the real ROM (in the case study: the tune
    routine, which calls BEEP; and the enemy decision source, which reads
    page 0, so enemy decisions become deterministic rather than authentic);
  - what is unaffected (TIMING -- which is the property every timing
    measurement rests on, because a substituted ROM changes what bytes are
    read, not how long the reads take).

If gameplay DOES call the ROM -- print, keyboard, calculator, BEEPER, CLS
-- use a real ROM image locally. Otherwise your measurements are fiction
and 6.2's "timing is unaffected" claim is false, because the timing of the
substituted routine is nothing like the real one's. Ship neither the image
nor a transcription of its behaviour (0.5).

RULE: substituting is legitimate IF AND ONLY IF you enumerate what depends
on it and state which measurements survive.

6.3 THE INTERRUPT TRAP
......................

This generalises exactly, so learn it once:

  - START WITH INTERRUPTS DISABLED. A game typically does not select its
    interrupt mode until partway through setup; an interrupt accepted
    before then dispatches in the previous mode -- often IM 1 to $0038 --
    straight into the ROM you may not have.
  - RUN TO THE FIRST INSTRUCTION AFTER THE MODE IS SELECTED. ASSERT the
    mode register is what you expected. THEN enable. In the case study:
    run to $A420 (just past LD I,A / IM 2 at $A41E), assert IM == 2, set
    IFF.
  - BUT YOU MUST ENABLE. ISR-driven countdowns are what pause, death and
    transition routines spin on. In the case study, running without
    interrupts deadlocked on the first attempt.
  - VERIFY THE IM 2 VECTOR TABLE PROPERLY: dump all 257 bytes at I*256 and
    count them (3.6). Two agreeing bytes prove nothing.

6.4 REGISTERS, STEPPING AND PROFILING
......................................

  - Write down your library's register index layout in a comment. In the
    case study, using SkoolKit: 6=H, 7=L, 12=SP, 13=I, 24=PC, 25=T-states,
    26=IFF, 27=IM.
  - Provide run(stop=ADDR) as the fast path, and IMPLEMENT YOUR OWN
    single-step that honours interrupt acceptance -- typically
    "if IFF and (T mod frame_duration) < int_active: accept_interrupt()".
  - On top of those, build:
      step_until(targets)  -- stop at any of a set of addresses
      profile(n)           -- histogram the PC over n instructions
    step_until should take a SAMPLE STRIDE for expensive predicates (the
    case study checks the level buffer every 2,000 instructions, not every
    instruction).
  - PROFILE OVER A FEW HUNDRED THOUSAND INSTRUCTIONS WITH NO KEYS PRESSED
    IS HOW YOU FIND THE MAIN LOOP IN A GAME YOU HAVE NEVER SEEN. It is the
    first thing to run after boot. BUT PROFILE TWICE -- once with
    interrupts armed and once suppressed -- AND DIFF. For an ISR-driven
    game the hottest address with interrupts armed is the idle spin or the
    HALT, and the actual game sits in the histogram's tail; the diff pulls
    the ISR's work out cleanly. Better still, bucket each sample by whether
    it was taken inside an accepted interrupt (track the ISR entry address
    and its RETI).
  - PERFORMANCE. Booting to a live level is millions of instructions and
    pure-Python simulation is slow. The case study runs with
    config={'fast_djnz': True, 'fast_ldir': True} and a 4,000,000
    instruction limit on the boot, which takes tens of seconds. Budget for
    that: a differential test that reboots per transition will not finish.
    Boot once, then poke.
  - A MEMORY-WRITE TRACER IS NOT USUALLY PROVIDED and you will need one
    (phase 2, Q3, and the display-file read-watch in 3.11). SkoolKit's
    tracer interface exposes read_port and write_port only. Either subclass
    the memory object and record (PC, address, value) on every write, or
    snapshot-and-diff at intervals. The subclass costs roughly 2x on speed;
    the snapshot diff costs nothing per instruction but misses writes that
    are later overwritten. Use the subclass for the one-off scans and the
    snapshot diff for "what changed across this transition".

6.5 THE PORTS TRACER (INPUT AND MEASUREMENT IN ONE OBJECT)
...........................................................

Getting this object wrong produces the "precise nonsense" that G2 warns
about, and it is wrong in ways that never announce themselves. The MINIMUM
correct read handler:

    def read_port(self, registers, port):
        if port & 0xFF == 0xFE:                  # keyboard / EAR
            hi = port >> 8
            if hi == 0:                          # B=0: ALL half-rows
                return AND of every stored value, else 0xFF
            v = 0xFF
            for select, val in self.pressed.items():
                if hi & select == 0 or hi == select:
                    v &= val                     # only matching half-rows
            return v
        if port & 0xFF == 0x1F:                  # Kempston: ACTIVE HIGH
            return self.kempston                 # default 0x00, not 0xFF
        return 0xFF

Four things there, each of which is a real trap:

  - THE HALF-ROW DISPATCH IS NOT OPTIONAL. Return the stored mask for every
    keyboard read and you press phantom keys on all eight half-rows.
  - B = 0 SELECTS ALL EIGHT and returns their AND. It is the standard "is
    any key pressed" idiom; a handler that only matches single half-rows
    returns $FF for it and the game concludes nothing is pressed, forever.
  - KEMPSTON IS ACTIVE HIGH, so a blanket $FF default holds
    up+down+left+right+fire on every read. Default it to $00 -- unless the
    game probes for the absence of the interface, in which case $FF is what
    tells it there is none. Q2 tells you whether $1F is even read.
  - TO PRESS A KEY, RETURN THE MASK THE GAME STORED, VERBATIM (3.4).

UNATTACHED PORTS AND ENTROPY. A constant $FF is a lie that degenerates some
games silently. If the game draws entropy from the floating bus
(IN A,($FF)), the R register or FRAMES, a constant makes it deterministic
in a way the real machine is not -- exactly E1's problem one level lower.
Decide per game: reproduce the floating bus roughly (it returns display
bytes during the pixel-fetch window and $FF otherwise), or feed a
deterministic pattern and DECLARE IT, as the case study declared its dummy
ROM page 0. Note also that a game syncing to the raster with IN A,($FF)
will spin forever against a constant. Whatever you choose, timing
measurements stay valid as long as the substitute takes the same time --
which it does, because port reads are fixed cost.

SUBCLASS THE SAME TRACER TO MEASURE OUTPUT: override write_port and append
(T-state, port, value) on every call, or only on changes of the bit you
care about. That is the entire sound measurement rig, in five lines; the
same object is the port inventory of Q2, the border-effect detector of
3.11's model 4, and the AY register logger of phase 11.

6.6 CALLING A ROUTINE IN ISOLATION
...................................

The basic recipe, for a leaf routine whose contract is (registers in) ->
(memory or port effects out):

  1. set PC to the routine's address;
  2. set the registers it reads;
  3. FABRICATE RETURN ADDRESSES -- see below;
  4. run until you hit one, then diff the T-state counter.

THE NAIVE VERSION -- one fabricated return at SP-2 with a HALT byte written
at it -- is unsound in three common cases, one of which is this manual's
own headline finding:

  (a) STACK UNWINDING (C1). The game's idiom for "the player dies" or
      "level complete" is discarding return addresses with extra POPs. With
      a single fabricated return, the POPs pull garbage off your stack and
      the RET goes anywhere.
  (b) SP AS A FAST DATA POINTER (3.10). LD SP,table destroys your sentinel.
  (c) SELF-CHECKING CODE detects the $76 byte you wrote into the image.

THE ROBUST VERSION, which is barely more work and turns (a) from a hazard
into a measurement:

  - Push a short run of DISTINCT sentinel addresses (say six), so that
    however many returns the routine discards, it lands on one of yours.
  - IMPLEMENT THE BREAKPOINTS AS PC COMPARISONS IN THE SIMULATOR, NEVER AS
    BYTE PATCHES. Never write into the image you are measuring.
  - REPORT WHICH SENTINEL WAS REACHED. That number is the routine's exit
    code: "returned normally", "abandoned one frame", "signalled death".
    You now have the control-flow information for free, on every call.

6.7 DRIVING TO A LIVE LEVEL, AND FINDING THINGS IN RAM
.......................................................

Phase 8 is the procedure; the harness pieces are:

  - MENU KEY DISCOVERY: single-step from boot with no keys and stop at the
    first port read with low byte $FE. Log the half-row select and read the
    following instructions for the bit tested. Repeat for each menu screen.
  - STRUCTURE DISCOVERY BY CONTENT SEARCH: scan all 64K for a window
    matching bytes you already decoded. Report the address. That is how you
    find the live level buffer with no reference.
  - STRUCTURE DISCOVERY BY TRANSITION DIFF: snapshot, cause one thing to
    happen, snapshot, list changed bytes. That is how you find entity
    slots, indices and pointers.
  - SYNCHRONISE ON EXTRACTED DATA, not on an instruction count: single-step
    and, every couple of thousand instructions, compare the first 32 bytes
    of the live level buffer against your statically decoded level. Robust,
    and it returns an instruction count that regressions will change.

6.8 CONSTRUCTING SCENARIOS BY POKING
.....................................

Once you can reach a live level, you can ask the original almost anything:

  - put the player in free fall by writing position, airborne flag,
    velocity and phase, then record T-states per pixel of descent;
  - poke items out of the level, drop an enemy onto the player, single-step
    to the death routine, run to respawn, and recount -- that is how "death
    keeps collected items" was established;
  - place the player at every x across a platform edge and enumerate the
    outcomes;
  - break INSIDE a routine at chosen PCs and record what it RESOLVED rather
    than what it returned -- in the case study, reading the sprite index
    register partway through a draw routine is what turned "I think the
    sub-cell x is discarded" into a fact.

6.9 THE DIFFERENTIAL TEST, IN EIGHT STEPS
..........................................

This is the strongest check available to you. Build one for every subsystem
that has a clean call boundary.

  1. BRACKET THE CALL SITE, NOT THE ROUTINE. Pick the CALL instruction's
     address and the address it returns to. In the case study, $9946 and
     $9949 around CALL $911E. Bracketing the call site captures the routine
     in the state the game actually puts it in, and captures any stack
     tricks it plays on its caller.
  2. BOOT TO A LIVE LEVEL through the game's own menus, and cross-check
     live RAM against your static tables BEFORE measuring anything.
  3. SNAPSHOT EVERY INPUT THE ROUTINE CAN READ, PER TRANSITION. Registers,
     the entity slots, the round-robin index, the random-source pointer,
     AND THE MUTABLE WORLD -- in the case study, all 672 bytes of the level
     buffer. That last one is the one people omit, and omitting it makes
     every item-eating transition pass silently for the wrong reason (G4).
  4. STEP TO THE RETURN ADDRESS AND SNAPSHOT AGAIN.
  5. REPLAY EACH "BEFORE" THROUGH YOUR MODEL, INDEPENDENTLY. Re-seed from
     the captured state every time so there is no drift.
  6. COMPARE EVERY FIELD, INCLUDING MEMORY. Report the FIRST divergence
     with full context -- and break out the inputs that drove the decision,
     for example the random byte's individual bits, so the report tells you
     which instruction to go and read.
  7. PRINT THE PATH HISTOGRAM (G3), labelling any line that is a sub-count
     rather than a path.
  8. THEN DO IT AGAINST THE CODE YOU SHIP. THIS STEP IS USUALLY SKIPPED AND
     IT IS HALF THE VALUE. A model written in the harness's language
     (Python) proves nothing about a JavaScript engine, however carefully
     it mirrors the same instructions -- and the case study's own headline
     "900/900" is exactly this: a Python reimplementation of $911E/$9178/
     $91F3, not the shipped artifact. The fix is half a page:
        - dump every captured (before, after) transition to JSON;
        - in Node, load the BUILT artifact, reach the engine's update
          function through the phase-7.6 test hook, seed it from each
          "before" record, run one update, and read back the same fields;
        - diff there, and print the same histogram.
     Do that and R8 covers your artifact. Skip it and you must report the
     result as "the model is exact", never "the engine is exact".

WHAT IT PROVES: your model is exact on the paths exercised, transition by
transition, with an oracle at every step and a realistic input
distribution.
WHAT IT DOES NOT PROVE: anything about paths that did not run; anything
downstream of a substituted environment; and, without step 8, anything
about the shipped code.

A REFINEMENT THAT MATTERS: model the ABANDONED UPDATE. In the case study,
eating an item pops an extra return address, and the NEXT update sees a
state value above the normal range and subtracts an offset -- so eating
costs two updates. Get that wrong and the differential fails on exactly
four transitions out of nine hundred.

6.10 PRINT THE SAME TABLE FROM BOTH SIDES
..........................................

Where a differential trace is overkill, make the Z80 tool and the engine
tool TAKE THE SAME ARGUMENTS AND PRINT THE SAME TABLE, so they can be
diffed directly and kept as a regression test. In the case study
"sim_edgejump.py 55 228 240" and "cmp_edgejump.js 55 228 240" print twelve
rows each, and the twelve rows are identical. This is cheap, it is
cross-language by construction, and it is the poor relation of 6.9 step 8
that you can build in an afternoon.

7. HOW TO STRUCTURE THE JAVASCRIPT ENGINE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

7.1 MIRROR THE ORIGINAL'S DISPATCH STRUCTURE EXACTLY
.....................................................

    function onePass() {          // one turn of the Z80 main loop
        // ... the work the original does every pass ...
        if (--frameCtr === 0) { frameCtr = RELOAD; longPath(); }
    }

    function longPath() {         // the work gated on the counter wrapping
        if (--liftDiv === 0)  { liftDiv  = 2;  updateLifts();  }
        if (--bonusDiv === 0) { bonusDiv = 10; tickBonus();    }
        if (--enemyDiv === 0) { enemyDiv = enemySpeed(); updateOneEnemy(); }
        // ...
    }

That shape is the case study's, because the case study is free-running with
a long path. IF YOUR GAME IS FRAME-SYNCED the outer shape is an ISR-shaped
update called once per frame, and if it is ISR-DRIVEN the update IS the
ISR. What generalises is not the shape but the rule:

THE STRUCTURE IS NOT AN APPROXIMATION OF THE ORIGINAL'S. IT IS THE SAME
STRUCTURE. That is what makes a routine map possible, what makes a
differential test meaningful, and what makes every later claim checkable by
someone reading both side by side. If you find yourself "tidying" the
dispatch into something more natural, you are destroying the property the
whole method depends on.

Quote the original beside the code it became:

    // $B34C MovePlayerToEndOfPlatform
    //   LD A,(farmer_pos_x) / AND 7 / RET z   <- skips on aligned x
    if ((px & 7) === 0) return;

This makes review possible, makes quirks survive refactoring, and is the
only way a later reader can distinguish a faithful oddity from a mistake.

7.2 TIME: SPEND T-STATES, NOT FRAMES AND NOT PASSES
....................................................

For a FREE-RUNNING game:

    passAcc += dt * CPU_HZ;                      // CPU_HZ = 3_500_000
    if (passAcc > CPU_HZ * 0.25) passAcc = CPU_HZ * 0.25;  // stall clamp
    while (passAcc > 0) {
        const cost = airborne ? T_AIRBORNE : T_GROUNDED;   // 560 : 406
        passAcc -= cost;
        simT += cost;                            // THE engine's real clock
        onePass();
    }

Three things here are load-bearing:

  - CHARGE EACH PASS ITS TRUE COST (B2). If any state changes which
    branches run, that state changes the game's speed.
  - CLAMP THE ACCUMULATOR so that a stall (a tab backgrounded, a garbage
    collection, a breakpoint) cannot spiral into a thousand catch-up
    passes.
  - simT IS THE ONLY CLOCK ANYTHING NON-VISUAL MAY USE. Sound scheduling,
    run timers, recording, comparisons across machines: all keyed to simT.
    Only drawing is keyed to the display.

For a FRAME-SYNCED game you do not need per-path costs, but YOU STILL NEED
THE ACCUMULATOR, and this is exactly where B6's trap lives. "Run a fixed
50.08 Hz timestep" must NOT be "one update per requestAnimationFrame":

    acc += dt;                                   // dt in seconds
    if (acc > 0.25) acc = 0.25;                  // same stall clamp
    while (acc >= 1/50.08) { acc -= 1/50.08; oneFrame(); frames++; }
    // rAF drives DRAWING only, never the update count

On a 60 Hz display, one update per rAF is a 20% speed error and it looks
completely right. On 144 Hz it is 188% fast. And see B2b for reproducing
the original's dropped frames, which a rigid timestep never does.

For an ISR-DRIVEN game the unit of time is the ISR invocation and the same
accumulator applies with the same clamp; the main loop, if there is one, is
a spin you can ignore.

7.3 COORDINATES
...............

  - Keep the GAME'S coordinate system in the state, not the screen's.
    Convert only in the renderer. If the game's y is bottom-up, your state
    is bottom-up.
  - Convert PER DRAW ROUTINE (D8), not per game.
  - Mask every 8-bit quantity on every write (C5). Write a helper if it
    helps you remember.

7.4 STATE AND DETERMINISM
.........................

  - THE SIMULATION MUST BE DETERMINISTIC given (initial state, input
    sequence). This is not a nicety: it is what makes replay tests,
    differential tests and bug reproduction possible.
  - Your substituted random source must be seeded from state you control
    and included in every snapshot.
  - ENUMERATE YOUR STATE BY FINGERPRINTING, NOT BY MEMORY (G10). Write a
    function that flattens everything the simulation reads or writes into
    one comparable value, and assert:
        run 30 -> mark; run 90 -> later;
        rewind 90 -> state equals mark EXACTLY;
        run 90 -> state equals later EXACTLY.
    A determinism property is always stronger than an equality property.
  - Every accumulator with a remainder is state (B7).
  - Record INPUT TRANSITIONS stamped with the simulation's own pass
    counter, not positions sampled per frame (B6). Write down at both the
    record and the playback site whether the stamp is before or after the
    step (G9).

7.5 RENDERING
.............

  - Draw monochrome bitmaps, then apply one ink per cell -- unless 3.3
    says otherwise for your game.
  - Never carry colour in sprite data unless the sprite routine writes
    attributes (3.3 (i)).
  - THE RENDERER MAY USE ONLY PRIMITIVES A 30-LINE STUB CAN RASTERIZE.
    fillRect with a solid fillStyle is enough for a Spectrum port and it is
    what the case study uses everywhere. No drawImage, no putImageData, no
    offscreen canvas, no CSS filters, no WebGL. This is not stylistic: it
    is what makes phase 14's headless pixel diff, draw-call capture and
    filmstrip buildable at all, and you cannot retrofit it.
  - The render function must be callable with no side effects on state, so
    a test can render twice and diff the draw lists (G6).

7.6 THE TEST HOOK
.................

Export exactly ONE object as the entire test surface, and design it in from
the start:

    globalThis.__GAME__ = { Game, update, render, onePass, constants, ... };

It must expose enough to SEED the simulation from an arbitrary state, not
just to read it, or 6.9 step 8 is impossible. It is the only reason phases
13 to 15 work. Retrofitting it is painful and you will under-expose.

7.7 PACKAGING
.............

  - Build the shipped single file from a TEMPLATE. Say loudly, in the
    generated file's first line, that it is generated and which file to
    edit.
  - Inline data as JSON inside a script element of a non-executable type,
    escaping the sequence "</" so the payload cannot break out -- then
    RE-PARSE the escaped text in the build step to prove the escaping held,
    and fail the build if a placeholder is missing.
  - Load THE BUILT FILE in your headless tests so the build step is itself
    under test.

8. IS THIS GAME LIKE THE EXAMPLE? -- THE DIAGNOSTIC BATTERY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The method above is legible enough that you may be tempted to EXECUTE it
rather than TEST it. Do not. Each diagnostic below tells you whether an
assumption baked into the pipeline holds for YOUR game, and what to do if
it does not.

THE BATTERY IS IN TWO HALVES AND THAT MATTERS. Q1-Q9 need only
the tape and a disassembler; run them on day one, and on an unprotected
single-screen game each is under an hour. Q10-Q19 NEED THE HARNESS
(phase 7) and most of them need scripted input into live play (phase 8);
run them as a phase-8 checkpoint. Two of them are not an hour at all: Q1 on
a protected, multi-stage-encrypted game is days, and Q6 (no reference
disassembly) is a standing tax on everything else.

Fill in the decision checklist at 8.20 before writing any engine code.

              ---- PRE-HARNESS: TAPE AND STATIC IMAGE ----

Q1  TAPE STRUCTURE AND PROTECTION
    DO: list every block ID and length; verify every checksum; look for a
        CALL $0556 in the loader.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: one text block and four standard-speed 0x10 blocks,
        all checksums clean, stock ROM loader. Nothing to defeat.
    A DIFFERENT ANSWER LOOKS LIKE: 0x15 (direct recording), 0x18 (CSW) or
        0x19 (generalized data) -- these genuinely defeat a block parser.
        Or a loader with its own edge-detection loop instead of a ROM call.
        Or a payload the loader decrypts or de-interleaves in RAM. Note
        that 0x11 (turbo) and 0x14 (pure data) do NOT belong on this list:
        both carry explicit data-length fields and parse exactly like 0x10;
        only the pulse timings differ, and you are not reproducing the load.
    WHAT CHANGES: you go to the snapshot route (6.1). DO NOT reverse the
        protection. Break at the transfer into the game in Fuse or ZEsarUX,
        or use tap2sna.py, export a .z80/.sna, and use that as your memory
        image. Then accept the consequence: R3 is unavailable and the pixel
        diff plus closure test carry the load. Budget a stage this manual's
        case study never had to build. Anything from 1985 onward should be
        assumed protected.

Q2  THE PORT INVENTORY                                   [RUN THIS FIRST]
    DO: log every distinct port touched by IN and OUT. Statically over the
        whole image (all opcode forms: DB/D3 and the ED 40-79 family), and
        then dynamically over a few million instructions once you have the
        harness. It costs nothing and it answers, in one run, questions
        that several later diagnostics are subsets of.
    WHAT IT TELLS YOU:
        $FE          keyboard, EAR, border, beeper (bit 4)
        $1F          Kempston joystick, ACTIVE HIGH
        $FFFD/$BFFD  AY-3-8912 -- present on a 48K game too, via an
                     interface (3.5)
        $7FFD        128K paging
        $FF          floating bus -- raster sync or entropy
        anything else  Interface 1/2, speech, printer, a peripheral you
                     have to decide about
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: $FE only, plus the IM 2 handler driving a Fuller
        Orator speech unit -- which is exactly the kind of port-visible
        peripheral this scan is for.
    WHAT CHANGES: Q15's raster question, Q17's sound architecture, Q8's
        machine question, and your harness's default return values (6.5).

Q3  SELF-MODIFICATION AND RUNTIME DECRYPTION
    DO: two watches, both one-off. (a) Flag any WRITE into a region that
        has ever been executed. (b) Flag EXECUTION of any region that was
        not present in your static image. Run both over driven play.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: neither fires. The binary is static.
    WHAT CHANGES: everything downstream, silently. If (a) fires, the
        routine's own bytes are part of the mutable world, so 6.9's
        per-transition replay re-seeds the wrong code; a coverage-based
        code/data map breaks in both directions (bytes written then
        executed look like data, patched operands look like changed code);
        and 6.6's byte-patch sentinel is detectable. If (b) fires, no
        single snapshot holds all the plaintext -- break on transfer of
        control INTO each region and dump it there. Run this BEFORE you
        trust Q13, phase 4, phase 10 or any differential test.

Q4  DOES THE GAME CALL THE ROM AT RUNTIME?
    DO: grep for CALL/JP/RST below $4000; grep for reads of $5C00-$5CB5;
        look for address arithmetic that can land below $4000 (3.7).
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: gameplay does not; the tune routine calls $2D28 and
        $03F8, the name-entry screen switches to IM 1 and uses KSTATE and
        LAST_K, and the enemy PRNG READS ROM page 0 as data.
    WHAT CHANGES: whether 6.2's ROM-less harness is viable at all. If
        gameplay calls ROM code, use a real ROM locally or your timings are
        fiction.

Q5  DOES THE GAME BLOCK RUN STANDALONE?
    DO: build the image without any loader side effects and run it.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: no -- it hangs instantly on a one-shot guard reading
        a system variable the loader wrote.
    WHAT CHANGES: you must enumerate and reproduce the loader's writes
        (phase 2, 6.1). Also check SP, the interrupt mode, and I.

Q6  DOES A REFERENCE DISASSEMBLY EXIST?
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: yes, and it was the project's single biggest
        advantage -- and its biggest over-fit. Three tools were driven
        directly off its control file: label resolution, annotated
        disassembly, and asset extraction (which read sprite dimensions out
        of its macros).
    USUALLY THERE WILL BE NONE. REPLACEMENTS, in order:
        - code/data separation -> execution coverage (Q13) plus recursive
          descent from the entry point plus a memory-read tracer;
        - graphic dimensions -> read them out of the DRAW ROUTINE'S inner
          loop;
        - graphic LOCATION -> a contact-sheet sweep (5.0a);
        - working-RAM structure names -> content search and transition diff
          (6.7);
        - byte-level extraction cross-check -> simply lost. Replace it
          end-to-end with the pixel diff and the closure test.
    WHAT CHANGES: phase 5 splits in two (5.0a/5.0b) and you budget several
        extra days, front-loaded. And even WITH a reference, do not trust
        its labels (C7).

Q7  THE LEVEL FORMAT
    DO: sweep candidate strides looking for a self-consistent grid; then
        run the CLOSURE TEST -- a known invariant satisfied exactly, with
        ZERO unrecognised values.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: raw uncompressed 32x21 tile indices, 8 levels at
        base $B3B0 with stride $2A0, exactly 12 EGGS each. (Corn is also
        collectible and varies 4 to 38 -- pick the invariant that is
        actually invariant.)
    A DIFFERENT ANSWER LOOKS LIKE: RLE on tile bytes, nybble packing,
        bit-packed 1bpp maps, per-level object lists over a shared
        background, screen$-as-level (the "map" is a compressed screen dump
        and collision is read back from the display file), procedural
        generation, or LEVELS KEPT COMPRESSED IN RAM and decompressed one
        at a time into a buffer -- which is common and which makes the
        closure test fail CORRECTLY while you keep hunting for a format
        that is not there.
    WHAT CHANGES: cap your time budget on format archaeology hard. FOR
        ANYTHING PACKED, THE PRIMARY PATH IS TO READ THE LEVELS OUT OF THE
        LIVE BUFFER in the simulator, level by level, poking the level
        counter between reads. It always works and it costs an afternoon.
        If there is no level buffer either -- object lists, or a
        framebuffer game (Q15) -- there is nothing to extract and phase 5
        is replaced entirely by phase 8 and phase 10.

Q8  MACHINE, PAGING, COMPRESSION, MULTI-LOAD
    DO: from Q2's port list plus a look for probes for 128K, a second tape
        load mid-game, and a decompressor at start-up.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: none of the above; one load, 48K, uncompressed.
    WHAT CHANGES: paging means your memory image is not 64K flat, and both
        the harness and the extraction need a bank model. Multi-load means
        several images and a level-transition mechanism. Compression means
        Q1's snapshot route rather than a block copy.

Q9  THE PRE-SHIFT TEST
    DO: for every pair of sprites that look like the same object, test
        whether one is a bit-shifted copy of the other over the middle
        rows. COUNT THE COPIES.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: yes, 4 pixels, on rows 3-11 of a 16-row sprite --
        two copies, and the shift exactly cancels the draw routine's
        discarded sub-cell x (F1).
    WHAT CHANGES: two very different things depending on the count. TWO
        copies of a cell-aligned sprite is usually a COMPENSATION, and
        "improving" the motion is a regression. FOUR OR EIGHT copies in a
        scrolling game is the horizontal scroll GRANULARITY (8 copies = 1
        px, 2 = 4 px, none = 8 px cell scroll) and your extractor is about
        to report 8x as many sprites as the game has. Run this BEFORE you
        touch anything that looks jerky.

              ---- POST-HARNESS: NEEDS PHASES 7 AND 8 ----

Q10 THE CLOCK: FREE-RUNNING, FRAME-SYNCED, ISR-DRIVEN, OR HYBRID?
                                                        [SHARPEST TEST]
    DO: HISTOGRAM the T-states between successive visits to the top of the
        main loop. Do not take the mean -- the shape of the distribution is
        the answer. Then run the corollary: suppress interrupts and see
        whether entities still move.
    HOW TO READ THE HISTOGRAM:
        a tight cluster at exactly one frame period (69,888 on 48K, 70,908
            on 128K)                      -> HALT-synced
        a cluster at 1x with a tail at 2x and 3x
                                          -> frame-synced WITH DROPPED
                                             FRAMES (see B2b -- you must
                                             reproduce or declare them)
        one frame period plus a variable remainder
                                          -> polls FRAMES or its own ISR
                                             flag; granularity is the poll
        a broad distribution in the hundreds or low thousands
                                          -> free-running
        no meaningful "top of the main loop" at all, because the main loop
            is a HALT or a two-instruction spin on an ISR-set flag
                                          -> ISR-DRIVEN. The question has
                                             no referent; the unit of time
                                             is the ISR invocation, and
                                             6.4's paired profile (armed
                                             versus suppressed) is how you
                                             find the real update.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: 399 T-states per pass measured over a live level --
        free-running, with the ISR driving a speech peripheral and one
        countdown that a death-wait spins on. So it needed BOTH clocks.
    NOTE ON SEPARATION: 69,888 / 399 is a factor of 175 in this game, which
        is why the answer was obvious here. It is not obvious in general --
        a game with a ~20,000 T-state pass is genuinely ambiguous against a
        two-frame 139,776 -- which is exactly why you histogram rather than
        compare a mean against a constant.
    WHAT CHANGES: EVERYTHING about your engine's time model (7.2). Also
        trace the ISR and list what it maintains, because a hybrid needs
        both clocks and a game with no ISR work at all still needs one
        armed if anything spins on it.

Q11 PER-PATH PASS COST
    DO: histogram the per-pass T-state cost over tens of thousands of
        passes and look for multi-modality (phase 9). Each mode is a branch
        you have not identified yet.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: two modes, 406 grounded and 560 airborne.
    WHAT CHANGES: whether you need per-path constants at all, and which
        states carry them. For a game whose hot code is below $8000 the
        modes will be smeared by contention and per-path constants stop
        being available (3.8).

Q12 ENTITY UPDATE-INTERVAL HISTOGRAM
    DO: log the pass number every time each entity's position changes, and
        histogram the intervals. Also log each slot's update count as a
        function of distance from the camera, if there is one.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: the divider chain of phase 10.2 -- 130 x per-entity
        dividers x round-robin over five slots.
    A DIFFERENT ANSWER LOOKS LIKE: 8.8 fixed-point speeds added every
        frame; move-every-N counters; update-all-with-DJNZ; or, in a
        scroller, entities outside the viewport not updated at all.
    WHAT CHANGES: with round-robin, entity speed is INDEPENDENT of how many
        are alive; with update-all it is not. Read the top of the entity
        routine to tell which.

Q13 CODE VERSUS DATA, FROM EXECUTION COVERAGE
    DO: run the game and mark every byte of every instruction executed --
        not just the PC byte, because operands are never a PC and marking
        only the PC fragments everything into single bytes. Then coalesce
        adjacent marks into regions.
    RULE: COVERAGE IS A LOWER BOUND AND ONLY AS GOOD AS THE PLAY THAT DROVE
        IT. Idle play will report the entire jump, fall and landing
        machinery as "data", because the player never jumped. Drive it:
        walk, jump, walk back, climb, die, complete a level. If the game
        ships an RZX recording, replay it for a free full-playthrough
        coverage map. And run Q3 first -- self-modifying code makes this
        diagnostic lie in both directions.
    NOTE: the case study never built this tool; it had a reference
        disassembly instead. There are no case-study numbers to quote here
        and you should distrust any that appear.

Q14 WHICH WAY IS UP?
    DO: find the screen-address routine (all three shapes -- 3.2); then
        score your decoded level BOTH ways against a real frame.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: bottom-up, by 96.3% to 35.8%.
    WHAT CHANGES: the score MARGIN is the gate, not the winner. If it is
        marginal, your classifier is broken, not the question.

Q15 THE RENDER MODEL: STATE-THEN-RENDER, FRAMEBUFFER, SCROLLING, RASTER?
    DO: four cheap tests, all from the harness.
        (a) BLITTER OPCODE: find the inner draw loop and read the combining
            instruction. OR = unmasked additive; AND-then-OR = masked, and
            your sprite data is two interleaved streams; XOR = erase by
            redraw.
        (b) DISPLAY-FILE READ-BACK: watch reads of $4000-$57FF during
            driven play. If gameplay code reads the screen to decide
            solidity, the framebuffer IS the game state.
        (c) SCROLLING: watch for bulk moves of screen memory (long LDIR
            runs or RL sweeps across $4000-$57FF), and for a camera
            variable that tracks the player.
        (d) RASTER TIMING: from Q2, repeated $FE writes at regular
            sub-frame intervals with the border bits changing.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: a bare OR (HL), no read-back, no scrolling, no
        raster effects -- model 1, the easiest case, and the one every gate
        in this manual is written for.
    WHAT CHANGES: see 3.11 in full. At minimum: (b) or (c) invalidates
        phase 5's tile extraction and phase 6's pixel gate as written; (d)
        means your renderer needs a scanline model.

Q16 BRIGHT, FLASH, AND WHERE COLOUR COMES FROM
    DO: scan every attribute constant the game writes; find the
        attribute-writing code; census the inks in a real frame; and check
        whether the SPRITE routine writes attributes too.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: all values below 64 -- BRIGHT never set -- with
        colour computed per tile at $9555 from the nine-entry table at
        $984F, ids >= 9 hard-coded to 6. No sprite-written attributes.
    WHAT CHANGES: your palette constants, whether you need a flash cycle,
        and -- if the attribute file carries the game state (3.3 (ii)) --
        which diff is your primary gate.

Q17 SOUND INVENTORY AND ARCHITECTURE
    DO: from Q2's port list plus the static and dynamic speaker-write scan
        (11.1). Then histogram the T-state gap between the first and last
        speaker write in each frame period to classify the architecture
        (11.3).
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: exactly two speaker writes in 18,900 bytes, both in
        one routine at $9CA4, blocking. Plus tunes through ROM BEEP, also
        blocking -- the original really does freeze for 4.8 seconds on
        every death.
    WHAT CHANGES: a per-frame slice, a multi-channel beeper engine or an AY
        path is a different order of problem AND A DIFFERENT ENGINE SHAPE,
        not just different audio. Phase 11 branches on this answer.

Q18 THE RANDOM SOURCE
    DO: find it; determine what it actually is (ROM walker, LFSR, R
        register, FRAMES, floating bus, LCG); and determine HOW ITS OUTPUT
        IS CONSUMED -- which bit, at what cadence, against what other
        periodic quantity in the game.
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: a ROM page-0 pointer walker at $9128, with two
        correlated decision bits taken from the SAME byte.
    WHAT CHANGES: your substitute's constraint is STATISTICAL, not exact
        (E1). Write the behavioural test that fails when it is violated.

Q19 THE STACK-IMBALANCE SCAN
    DO: prefer the DYNAMIC version, because it needs no routine map and
        routine maps are exactly what you lack: in the simulator, record SP
        at every CALL and at the matching RET, and flag every mismatch. The
        static version -- counting PUSH against POP per routine -- needs
        boundaries you do not have, and routines fall through into each
        other anyway (C2).
    CASE STUDY ANSWER: several imbalances, and each was a game rule --
        death, level complete, abandon-this-update (C1).
    WHAT CHANGES: nothing about architecture, but it hands you a
        prioritised list of the routines whose control flow you MUST read
        carefully. Run it early; it is cheap and it is the one scan that
        finds rules you did not know existed. USELESS ON COMPILED BASIC,
        where it produces near-total false positives (3.11).

9. A WORKED MICRO-EXAMPLE, CARRIED END TO END
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

One rule, from "I wrote something plausible" to "it is pinned by a test
carrying the original's own answer". All addresses and numbers are the case
study's real ones, at ORG $8214. Read this as the shape of every rule you
will recover.

THE RULE: what happens when the player walks off the end of a platform.

STEP 1 -- THE PLAUSIBLE VERSION (what you must not ship)

    // check the ground under his feet each step
    if (tileUnderFeet(px, py) < SOLID) { falling = true; vy = 0; }

It is what any designer would specify. It produces a playable game. It
passes every screenshot test. It is wrong in three separate ways.

STEP 2 -- THE REPORT

A player who knew the original said: "Harry falls off the edge slightly
early." That sentence -- imprecise, unfalsifiable, not a specification --
is the entire reason anyone went looking. Treat it as a POINTER, never as a
fix. Nobody tuned a constant.

STEP 3 -- FIND THE ROUTINE AND READ ITS BYTES

From the annotated disassembler: $B34C, called from $99D6, and only when
the on-lift flag is zero. Twenty-one instructions, $B34C-$B380. This is the
real disassembly, not a paraphrase (tools/adis.py 0xB34C 40):

    $B34C  3ad872     LD A,(farmer_pos_x)
    $B34F  e607       AND 7
    $B351  c8         RET z           ; <-- NOTHING happens on aligned x
    $B352  2ad872     LD HL,(farmer_pos_x)
    $B355  cd349e     CALL $9E34      ; tile lookup -> HL
    $B358  013f00     LD BC,0x3f
    $B35B  a7         AND A
    $B35C  ed42       SBC HL,BC       ; two rows down, one column right
    $B35E  7e         LD A,(HL)       ; the tile under his feet
    $B35F  fe05       CP 5
    $B361  d0         RET nc          ; support is >= 5, INCLUDING the cage
    $B362  fe01       CP 1
    $B364  c8         RET z
    $B365  fe02       CP 2
    $B367  c8         RET z           ; ladder halves support too
    $B368  3e01       LD A,1
    $B36A  322573     LD (farmer_airbourne),A   ; a TRANSIENT state
    $B36D  16ff       LD D,0xff
    $B36F  3adb72     LD A,(farmer_direction)
    $B372  a7         AND A
    $B373  2002       JR nz,$B377
    $B375  1601       LD D,1
    $B377  7a         LD A,D          ; note: D is built, THEN moved to A
    $B378  322673     LD (farmer_jump_dir),A
    $B37B  3e04       LD A,4
    $B37D  322773     LD (farmer_jump_phase),A  ; a four-tick hesitation
    $B380  c9         RET

Note two things about the transcription itself, because both are traps this
manual has watched people fall into. "SBC HL,$3F" is not a Z80 instruction
-- SBC HL takes BC, DE, HL or SP only, so the real code is LD BC / AND A /
SBC HL,BC. And the direction value is built in D and only then moved to A;
a shorthand note that reads "LD D,$FF ... LD (farmer_jump_dir),A" omits the
LD A,D and is the line a reader would copy wrong. IF YOUR SPEC IS A
PARAPHRASE, LABEL IT AS ONE AND PUT THE TOOL INVOCATION BESIDE IT.

STEP 4 -- READ ALL OF IT, INCLUDING WHAT IT DOES NOT DO

Three findings the plausible version could never have produced:

  (a) AND 7 / RET z. The check is SKIPPED ENTIRELY on cell-aligned x. That
      gives exactly one pixel of overhang per platform edge. Checking every
      step drops him a pixel early -- which is precisely what the player
      reported. (Failure mode D1, and idiom (d) from phase 10.)
  (b) CP 5 / RET nc. Support is ">=", so the decorative birdcage tiles
      ($A8-$B5) hold him up while eggs (3) and corn (4) do not. (Failure
      mode D5.)
  (c) It sets airbourne = 1 and phase = 4, and $A294 only decrements that
      phase on the frameCtr == 1 pass -- so the transient lasts FOUR LONG
      PASSES before the fall proper begins, after which $A29F zeroes the
      horizontal velocity and he drops straight down. The dispatch at $A21C
      is three-way and its third arm (frameCtr != 1 AND airbourne == 1)
      does NOTHING AT ALL. That empty arm is the teeter. (Failure mode C3.)

STEP 5 -- FIND THE INTERACTION WITH ITS NEIGHBOUR

$B34C answers "can he stand here". $A356 answers "can he land here". They
read THE SAME CELL with DIFFERENT predicates:

    $A356: tile 0        -> keep falling
           tile 5        -> land
           tile >= 3     -> KEEP FALLING (so the birdcage supports standing
                           but does not catch a fall)
           tile 1 or 2   -> land only if the ADJACENT half's cell is floor
           plus (y+1) mod 8 == 0     -> the 8-pixel landing snap

Combined with (a), there is exactly one pixel per platform edge where the
player stands on nothing AND FROM WHICH A VERTICAL JUMP CANNOT LAND.

This was not noticed by inspiration. A static tool, check_stand_land.py,
enumerated EVERY position on EVERY level where $B34C says "stand" and
$A356 says "cannot land". That turns "I noticed an odd case" into the
complete list of odd cases. (Failure mode D4.)

STEP 6 -- ASK THE ORIGINAL, EXHAUSTIVELY, OVER THE WHOLE NEIGHBOURHOOD

Not at the one interesting x. At every x across the edge. Level 1, the
right-hand end of the memory-row-4 platform, y = 55:

    tools/sim_edgejump.py 55 228 240

drives the real Z80: boot to the level, poke the player to (x, 55), press
jump, run until he lands, record the landing y. Its three printed columns
are x, x&7, support and the landing y; the "verdict" column below is an
ANNOTATION added when writing this up, not tool output:

     x    x&7  support  [annotation]               vertical jump lands at
    231     7     5     supported                  y=55
    232     0     0     SKIPPED -> stays grounded  y=23   <- the perch
    233     1     0     falls                      y=23
    238     6     0     falls*                     y=55
    239     7     0     falls*                     y=55

Twelve rows in total for 228..239: 228-231 land at y=55, 232-237 at y=23,
238-239 back at y=55. Positions 238 and 239 are saved by a completely
different rule -- at x >= $EE the airborne horizontal routine at $A240
forces the velocity to $FF, carrying him back over the platform. (Do not
confuse that with the PLATFORM-edge bounce at $A32C, which reverses the
velocity with XOR $FE when the neighbouring cell is floor and the sub-cell
offset is past a threshold -- 4 moving left, 3 moving right. Two different
rules, both visible in this one table.) You would never have thought to
include either in a hand-picked test case. THAT is why you enumerate the
range.

STEP 7 -- MAKE THE ENGINE PRINT THE SAME TABLE

    node tools/cmp_edgejump.js 55 228 240

takes the same arguments and prints the same twelve rows from the JS
engine. All twelve identical, including 238 and 239. Diff the two outputs;
if they differ, the first differing row names the x to investigate.

STEP 8 -- FREEZE IT

In the headless suite, with the provenance in the comment so that nobody
"fixes" it later:

    // Level 1, right-hand end of the mem-row-4 platform, y=55.
    // Verified against the real Z80 (tools/sim_edgejump.py 55 228 240):
    //   228-231 -> land y=55, 232-237 -> land y=23, 238-239 -> land y=55.
    check("edge jump 232 (aligned perch)", jumpFrom(232, 55), 23);

WHAT THE RULE COST, AND WHAT IT BOUGHT

The plausible one-liner became: one skipped check, one ">=" predicate with
an explicit tile range, one transient state with a four-pass duration, one
empty dispatch arm, one separate landing predicate with an alignment
requirement, and one screen-edge velocity forcing. Six behaviours, every
one visible in play, and together they are what the platform edge FEELS
like in the original. Against the DONE rule (5.2): provenance at $B34C,
$A356, $A21C, $A294, $A29F, $A240; measured rather than reasoned; rung R7
over an enumerated neighbourhood; frozen with an expected value from the
simulator and the invocation recorded; limits declared.

A SECOND, SHORTER EXAMPLE, BECAUSE IT SHOWS MEASUREMENT RATHER THAN LOGIC

The beeper routine at $9CA4, eleven instructions:

    LD A,$10 / OUT ($FE),A / LD B,H / DJNZ $ /
    XOR A    / OUT ($FE),A / LD B,H / DJNZ $ /
    DEC L / JR nz / RET

Hand-counting gives you a symmetric square wave and gets two things wrong:
the loop tail (DEC L, JR, LD A) falls ENTIRELY on the low half, and H=0
means 256 because DJNZ with B=0 wraps. So:

    high   = 13H + 14
    low    = 13H + 33
    period = 26H + 47
    freq   = 3,500,000 / (26H + 47)

That model was NOT derived on paper. extract_sound.py calls the routine in
isolation (6.6) with HL set, records every speaker edge with its T-state
stamp, and verify() RAISES BEFORE WRITING ANYTHING if the model disagrees
for H in {0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 15, 30, 37, 40, 63, 100, 200, 255} -- asserting
both half-spans AND that there are exactly 8 edges for L=4, because the
edge count catches an off-by-one that the spans do not. Then render_sound.js
stubs the browser audio API with a recorder, drives THE SHIPPED ENGINE
through a scripted session, mixes what was actually scheduled to a WAV, and
the frequencies are measured back: every effect within 1% on pitch and
duration. Model -> engine -> rendered output -> measured back. That is
rungs R6 and R9 closed against each other.

11. THE FIVE HABITS, IF YOU REMEMBER NOTHING ELSE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  1. BUILD THE VERIFICATION HARNESS FIRST. Every plausible-looking routine
     is a hypothesis until the original agrees with it. You cannot hold
     that standard without a harness, and with one it costs almost nothing.

  2. QUOTE THE ORIGINAL IN THE PORT. The Z80 in a comment beside the code
     it became, with its address. It makes review possible, makes quirks
     survive refactoring, and is the only way a later reader can tell a
     faithful oddity from a mistake.

  3. PREFER MEASUREMENT TO DERIVATION, AND SAY WHICH YOU USED. Where they
     disagree, ship the measurement and log the disagreement as open.

  4. MAKE THE SANITY CHECK HUMAN-SCALE AND INDEPENDENT. "3.8 seconds to
     cross the screen", "a two-minute level", "an 11-pixel rise plus an
     8-pixel snap just clears one character cell". Those catch an error of
     130x. Two algebraic derivations sharing a measured constant never
     will.

  5. WHEN THE CODE LOOKS WRONG, ASSUME IT IS RIGHT AND FIND OUT WHY. The
     original shipped. The thing you are about to correct is load-bearing
     until proved otherwise.
